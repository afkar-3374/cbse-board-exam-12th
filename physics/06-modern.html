1. Photoelectric effect

Q. State Einstein's photoelectric equation. Use it to explain (i) threshold frequency, (ii) why Kmax does not depend on intensity, (iii) instantaneous emission.3 M · case study in 8 papers

hν = φ₀ + Kmax  ⇒  Kmax = eV₀ = h(ν − ν₀)

Light consists of photons of energy hν. One photon is absorbed by one electron. Part of the energy (work function φ₀ = hν₀) frees the electron; the rest is its kinetic energy.

  1. Threshold frequency: if ν < ν₀, hν < φ₀ and no electron can escape, however intense the light. (Red light fails where blue works; IR will also fail.)
  2. Intensity: intensity = number of photons per second. More photons → more electrons → larger photocurrent, but each photon still has energy hν, so Kmax and stopping potential are unchanged.
  3. Instantaneous: a photon's energy is given to one electron in a single collision; no time is needed to accumulate energy (wave theory cannot explain this).

Photon properties: energy E = hν, momentum p = h/λ, rest mass zero, speed c, number of photons (not their energy) increases with intensity, energy and momentum are conserved in collisions.

Work function: minimum energy needed to free an electron from the metal surface. Alkali metals (low φ₀) respond even to visible light.

A–R trap: "Increasing intensity increases stopping potential" is false. Stopping potential depends only on frequency (and the metal).

Q. Standard numericals.2–3 M

Shortcut: E(eV) = 1240/λ(nm). h = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV s.

  • φ = 2.14 eV, λ = 300 nm: E = 4.13 eV → Kmax = 1.99 eV, V₀ ≈ 2 V; λ₀ = 1240/2.14 = 579 nm.
  • φ = 2.2 eV, λ = 400 nm: E = 3.1 eV → Kmax = 0.9 eV; doubling intensity: unchanged.
  • φ = 2.0 eV, ν = 8×10¹⁴ Hz: E = 3.31 eV → Kmax = 1.31 eV.
  • ν₀ = 5×10¹⁴, ν = 8×10¹⁴: Kmax = h(3×10¹⁴) = 1.24 eV.
  • λ₀ = 330 nm → φ = 3.76 eV; with λ = 220 nm, Kmax = 5.64 − 3.76 = 1.9 eV.
  • φ = 4.14 eV → λ₀ = 300 nm (3000 Å). φ = 3.315 eV → λ₀ = 375 nm. φ = 2.5 eV → ν₀ = 6×10¹⁴ Hz. φ = 2.2 eV → ν₀ = 5.3×10¹⁴ Hz.
  • φ = 2.4 eV, V₀ = 0.6 V → E = 3 eV → λ = 413 nm.
  • Sodium 2.3 eV and copper 4.5 eV: λ₀ ratio = 4.5 : 2.3 ≈ 2 : 1.
  • Photons 3.3 eV and 11.3 eV on φ = 2.3 eV: K = 1 eV and 9 eV → v ratio = 1 : 3.
  • ν₀ and stopping potential: V₀ at 2ν₀ → at 3ν₀ it becomes 2V₀. Doubling ν (not from ν₀) gives more than 2V₀.

2. Photoelectric graphs

Q. Draw photocurrent vs anode potential for (i) same frequency, different intensities, (ii) same intensity, different frequencies.3 M · very frequent

Saturation current depends on intensity; stopping potential depends on frequency. Curves with the same V₀ but different saturation → same frequency, different intensity. Different V₀ for the same intensity → different frequencies (or different materials).

Q. Draw the graph of stopping potential vs frequency. What do the slope and intercepts represent? Compare two metals.2–3 M · case study

V₀ = (h/e)ν − φ₀/e
  • Slope = h/e (same for every metal) → slope × e = h.
  • ν-intercept = threshold frequency ν₀.
  • V₀-intercept = −φ₀/e.

The metal whose line cuts the ν-axis further right has the larger work function and gives smaller Kmax for the same light. Changing the metal or the intensity does not change the slope.

3. de Broglie waves

Q. State de Broglie's hypothesis. Derive λ for an electron accelerated through V volts. Plot λ vs V.2–3 M

λ = h/p = h/mv = h/√(2mK) = h/√(2meV)  ⇒  λe = 1.227/√V nm

Moving matter has a wave associated with it; λ depends on momentum, not on charge.

  • V = 100 V → λ = 0.1227 nm. V = 150 V → 0.1 nm. V × 4 → λ/2 (150 → 600 V halves λ).
  • λ = 1.227 Å → V = 100 V.
  • 25 V vs 36 V → λ ratio 6 : 5.
  • Same KE: λ ∝ 1/√m → electron has the longer λ than a proton. Same V: λ ∝ 1/√(mq).
  • Same λ → same momentum → K ∝ 1/m: Kp : Kα = 4 : 1.
  • α with 4× KE of proton: λα/λp = 1/√(4 × 4) = 1/4.
  • Ball 0.1 kg at 6.63 m/s → λ = 10⁻³³ m (too small to observe, which is why we don't see wave nature of everyday objects). 150 g at 30 m/s → 1.47×10⁻³⁴ m.
  • Momentum of electron with λ = 0.1 nm: p = h/λ = 6.63×10⁻²⁴ kg m/s.
  • Freely falling body: v increases → λ decreases (A–R: A and R true, R explains A).

Electron microscope: electron λ (~0.1 nm) ≪ visible light λ, so it resolves much finer detail.

4. Rutherford (Geiger–Marsden) scattering

Q. Describe the α-scattering experiment, its observations and conclusions. Draw N vs θ.3 M

  • Most α-particles pass undeflected → atom is mostly empty space.
  • A few deflected through large angles; about 1 in 8000 bounces back (θ > 90°) → all positive charge and most mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus (~10⁻¹⁵ m).

Distance of closest approach: K = (1/4πε₀)(2e · Ze)/d →

d = (1/4πε₀) · 2Ze² / K

d ∝ 1/K ∝ 1/v² — doubling KE halves d. A particle of 3× mass and 2× charge at the same speed: K becomes 3×, charge product 2× → d × 2/3. d ∝ Ze (target charge).

Limitations of Rutherford's model: (1) an orbiting (accelerating) electron should radiate continuously and spiral into the nucleus — atom unstable; (2) it predicts a continuous spectrum, but atoms give line spectra. Bohr fixed both with stationary orbits and quantised transitions.

5. Bohr model & spectra

Q. State Bohr's postulates. Derive the radius of the nth orbit and the energy of the electron. Show E₁ = −13.6 eV.3–5 M

Postulates: (1) electrons revolve in certain stable (stationary) orbits without radiating; (2) only orbits with angular momentum mvr = nh/2π are allowed; (3) a photon of energy hν = Ei − Ef is emitted/absorbed on a jump between orbits.

Centripetal force from Coulomb: mv²/r = ke²/r² … (1) and mvr = nh/2π … (2).

From (2): v = nh/2πmr. Put in (1): m n²h²/(4π²m²r²) · (1/r) = ke²/r² →

rn = ε₀ n² h² / (π m e²) = 0.53 n² Å   ⇒ r ∝ n²
vn = e²/(2ε₀nh) = (2.2 × 10⁶/n) m/s   ⇒ v ∝ 1/n

KE = ½mv² = ke²/2r; PE = −ke²/r; total E = −ke²/2r →

En = − m e⁴ / (8 ε₀² n² h²) = −13.6/n² eV

Substituting constants for n = 1 gives −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = −13.6 eV. Total energy is negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus (A–R: A and R true, R explains A).

Relations: KE = −E, PE = 2E, KE/E = −1. E = −3.4 eV → KE = 3.4 eV, PE = −6.8 eV. E = −1.51 eV is n = 3; jump to ground state emits 12.09 eV → λ = 102.6 nm. Time period T ∝ n³, frequency of revolution ∝ 1/n³. Speed in n = 2 is half that in n = 1. v(H, n = 2) : v(He⁺, n = 3) = (1/2) : (2/3) = 3 : 4.

de Broglie explanation of stationary orbits: an orbit is allowed if a whole number of electron wavelengths fits its circumference: 2πr = nλ = nh/mv → mvr = nh/2π.

6. Nucleus: size, density, nuclear force

Q. How does nuclear radius depend on A? Show that nuclear density is independent of A.2–3 M

R = R₀ A^{1/3}, R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm  ⇒  ρ = A m / (4/3 π R₀³ A) = 3m / (4π R₀³)

A cancels → density is the same for all nuclei (≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³). Reason: nuclear force saturates, so each nucleon occupies the same volume.

Ratios: R(A = 4) : R(32) = 1 : 2. R(27) : R(125) = 3 : 5. Fission 2 : 1 → R ratio 2^{1/3} : 1.

Isotopes: same Z (¹²C, ¹⁴C). Isobars: same A. Isotones: same N (¹⁹⁸Hg has N = 118, ¹⁹⁷Au has N = 118).

Q. State properties of nuclear force. Draw the potential energy of a pair of nucleons vs separation and mark attractive and repulsive regions.2–3 M · ~6 papers

  • Strongest force in nature (~100 × Coulomb).
  • Short range (a few fm); saturates (acts only with nearest neighbours).
  • Charge-independent: n–n, p–p and n–p forces are about equal (so "∝ charge" is false).
  • Attractive for r > 0.8 fm; strongly repulsive for r < 0.8 fm (keeps nucleons from collapsing); minimum PE (equilibrium) near 0.8–1 fm.

7. Mass defect, binding energy, fission & fusion

Q. Define mass defect and binding energy. Calculate BE and BE/A for ⁴He and ¹⁶O.2–3 M · 6 papers

Δm = [Z mp + (A − Z) mn] − Mnucleus,   BE = Δm c² = Δm × 931.5 MeV

Mass defect is the difference between the mass of the separate nucleons and the nucleus; BE is the energy equivalent — the energy needed to break the nucleus into free nucleons (or released when it forms). 1 u = 931.5 MeV.

⁴He (M = 4.0015 u, mp = 1.0073, mn = 1.0087): Δm = 2(1.0073) + 2(1.0087) − 4.0015 = 0.0305 u → BE = 28.4 MeV, BE/A = 7.1 MeV. (With Δm = 0.0304 u → 28.3 MeV, 7.07 MeV/nucleon.) ⁵⁶Fe (8.8 MeV) is more stable.

¹⁶O (atomic mass 15.99493 u, use mH = 1.007825, mn = 1.008665): Δm = 0.13699 u → BE = 127.6 MeV, BE/A = 7.98 MeV.

BE of 160 MeV (A = 20) vs 320 MeV (A = 40): BE/A = 8 and 8 MeV → equally stable. Stability depends on BE/A, not total BE.

Q. Draw the binding energy per nucleon curve. State its features and use it to explain energy release in fission and fusion. (~15 papers)2–5 M

  • BE/A is low for light nuclei, rises steeply, peaks at ≈ 8.8 MeV near A = 56, then decreases slowly (≈ 7.6 MeV for ²³⁸U).
  • Nearly constant for 30 < A < 170 because nuclear force is short-range and saturates.
  • Higher BE/A → more stable (more energy needed per nucleon to pull it apart).
  • Fission: a heavy nucleus (W, A ≈ 240) splits into two medium nuclei of higher BE/A → total BE increases → energy released (~200 MeV per fission).
  • Fusion: light nuclei (Z) combine into a nucleus of higher BE/A → energy released. Energy per nucleon is larger in fusion because the curve rises much more steeply at low A.
  • Total mass of products is less than reactants (mass converted to energy).

W, X, Y, Z question: fission → W (heaviest, on the descending part); fusion → Z (lightest, on the steep rising part). Y (A ≈ 56) undergoes neither.

Fusion needs very high temperature (~10⁷ K) to give nuclei enough KE to overcome Coulomb repulsion.

Q-value examples: ²H + ²H → ³He + n: Δm = 2(2.0141) − (3.0160 + 1.0087) = 0.0035 u → 3.26 MeV released. ⁵⁶Fe → 2 ²⁸Al: Q = [55.93494 − 2(27.98191)] × 931.5 = −26.9 MeV < 0 → not energetically possible.

Fission example: ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n + energy. Fusion example: ²H + ²H → ³He + n + 3.27 MeV.

1. Charges, field lines, flux

Q. State Coulomb's law in vector form.1–2 M

F⃗₁₂ = (1/4πε₀) · q₁q₂ / r² · r̂₂₁

Force is along the line joining the charges; like charges repel, unlike attract. 1/4πε₀ = 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻².

Q. Why do two electric field lines never cross?1 M · A–R favourite

At the point of intersection there would be two tangents, i.e. two directions of E at one point — impossible, since the net field at a point has a unique direction.

Q. Define electric flux. A charge q is placed at the centre of a cube. Find the flux through (a) the whole cube, (b) one face, (c) two opposite faces.1–2 M

Φ = ∮ E⃗ · dA⃗   (SI unit N m² C⁻¹ or V m)

(a) q/ε₀   (b) q/6ε₀   (c) 2 × q/6ε₀ = q/3ε₀.

Charge outside a closed surface → net flux = 0. Moving a charge inside the surface or changing its radius does not change the flux.

Uniform field + closed cube: flux in = flux out, so total flux = 0.

Q. Two charges +2 μC and +8 μC are 30 cm apart. Where is the field zero?1 M

Between them, at x from 2 μC: 2/x² = 8/(30−x)² → (30−x)/x = 2 → x = 10 cm.

Rule: for like charges the null point is between them, nearer the smaller charge; for unlike charges it is outside, nearer the smaller charge.

2. Electric dipole

Q. Derive the electric field on the equatorial line of a dipole.3–5 M · very frequent

  1. |E₊| = |E₋| = (1/4πε₀) · q/(r² + a²)
  2. Components ⟂ to the axis cancel; components ∥ to the axis add: E = 2E₊ cos θ, with cos θ = a/√(r² + a²).
  3. E = (1/4πε₀) · 2qa/(r² + a²)^{3/2} = (1/4πε₀) · p/(r² + a²)^{3/2}
  4. For r ≫ a:
    E⃗ = − (1/4πε₀) · p⃗ / r³

Compare: on the axial line E = (1/4πε₀)·2p/r³ (along p⃗). So Eaxial = 2Eequatorial, and both ∝ 1/r³ (halving r → field becomes 8×).

Q. Derive the potential due to a dipole at a point on its axis.3 M

Point P at distance r from centre, on the side of +q: V = (1/4πε₀)[q/(r−a) − q/(r+a)] = (1/4πε₀) · 2qa/(r² − a²).

V = (1/4πε₀) · p/(r² − a²)  ≈  (1/4πε₀) · p/r²  (r ≫ a)

General point: V = (1/4πε₀)·p cos θ / r². On the equatorial line θ = 90° → V = 0 (but E ≠ 0).

Q. Torque and potential energy of a dipole in a uniform field. When is it in stable / unstable equilibrium?2–3 M

τ⃗ = p⃗ × E⃗,   τ = pE sin θ   |   U = −p⃗ · E⃗ = −pE cos θ

Derivation of U: work done in rotating from θ₀ = 90° to θ: W = ∫ pE sin θ dθ = −pE cos θ.

  • θ = 0°: U = −pE (minimum) → stable.
  • θ = 180°: U = +pE (maximum) → unstable.
  • Net force in a uniform field = 0; only a torque acts. In a non-uniform field there is a net force too.
  • Torque is half the maximum when sin θ = ½ → θ = 30°.

Example: p = 4 × 10⁻⁹ C m at 30° to E = 5 × 10⁴ N/C → τ = 4×10⁻⁹ × 5×10⁴ × ½ = 10⁻⁴ N m.

3. Gauss's law

Q. State Gauss's law.1 M

∮ E⃗ · dA⃗ = qenclosed / ε₀

The total flux through any closed surface equals 1/ε₀ times the net charge enclosed. A zero net flux does not mean E = 0 at every point of the surface (a classic A–R trap).

Q. Using Gauss's law, find E due to an infinitely long straight uniformly charged wire (linear charge density λ).3–5 M · very frequent

Flux only through the curved surface: E · 2πrl = λl/ε₀

E = λ / (2πε₀ r)   ⇒  E ∝ 1/r

Direction: radially outward for λ > 0. Graph of E vs r is a rectangular hyperbola.

Q. Using Gauss's law, find E due to an infinite plane sheet (surface charge density σ). Does it depend on distance?3–5 M · very frequent

Flux = EA + EA = 2EA; charge enclosed = σA → 2EA = σA/ε₀

E = σ / 2ε₀   (independent of distance)

It is independent of distance because field lines are parallel and do not spread out (the sheet is infinite).

Two parallel sheets +σ and −σ: between them E = σ/ε₀; outside E = 0. Sheets +σ and −2σ: superpose each sheet's σ/2ε₀ field with direction (away from +, towards −).

Q. Using Gauss's law, find E due to a uniformly charged thin spherical shell (a) outside, (b) inside. Plot E vs r.3–5 M · very frequent

(a) r > R: Gaussian sphere of radius r encloses q → E·4πr² = q/ε₀ → E = q/(4πε₀r²) — same as if all charge were at the centre.

(b) r < R: encloses no charge → E = 0.

Potential: V = q/(4πε₀R) everywhere inside (constant, not zero) and q/(4πε₀r) outside.

Q. Why is the electric field inside a conductor zero in electrostatic equilibrium?A–R · 7 papers

If any field existed inside, the free electrons would move (current would flow) until their redistribution cancels it. Consequences:

  • Net charge resides on the outer surface.
  • Potential is constant throughout the volume and equal to its surface value.
  • E just outside is normal to the surface (a tangential component would move charges): E = σ/ε₀.
  • Electrostatic shielding: the cavity in a conductor is field-free.

4. Potential & equipotential surfaces

Q. Relation between E and V. Find E if V = 3x, or V = x² − 2y².1–2 M

E = − dV/dr   (E⃗ = −∇V)

V = 3x → E = −3 V/m, i.e. 3 V/m along −x.
V = x² − 2y² → Eₓ = −2x, Ey = 4y. At (2, 1): E = (−4, 4) → 135° with +x axis.

E points in the direction of decreasing potential. In a uniform field along +x, VA > VB for B further along x; points on a line ⟂ to E have equal potential.

Q. Define equipotential surface. Draw them for (i) a point charge, (ii) a uniform field, (iii) a dipole, (iv) two equal positive charges.2–3 M

A surface on which every point has the same potential. Properties: no work is done moving a charge on it; E is always ⟂ to it; two equipotentials never intersect.

Why not equidistant for a point charge? E is stronger near the charge, so a fixed ΔV is covered in a smaller Δr (Δr = ΔV/E).

E need not be constant on an equipotential surface — e.g. for two equal charges the outer surface is crowded near the charges and spread out between them.

Q. Derive the potential energy of a system of charges. Three charges q, 2q, 3q at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side a.2–3 M

U = (1/4πε₀) Σpairs qiqj/rij

Bring q₁ (no work), then q₂ (W = kq₁q₂/r₁₂), then q₃ (W = kq₁q₃/r₁₃ + kq₂q₃/r₂₃).

q, 2q, 3q: U = k(2q² + 3q² + 6q²)/a = 11kq²/a.

For q, 2q, nq with U = 0: 2 + n + 2n = 0 → n = −2/3. For −q, Q, −q equally spaced (d) with U = 0: −Qq/d − Qq/d + q²/2d = 0 → Q:q = 1:4.

Q. Is it true that if V = 0 at a point then E = 0 there?2 M

No. At the midpoint of a dipole, V = kq/a − kq/a = 0 but E = 2kq/a² ≠ 0 (towards −q). Conversely, inside a charged shell E = 0 but V ≠ 0.

5. Capacitors & dielectrics

Q. Derive the capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor (a) with air, (b) with a dielectric slab of thickness t < d.3–5 M · very frequent

(a) E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A), V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A)

C₀ = Q/V = ε₀A/d

(b) V = E₀(d − t) + (E₀/K)t = (Q/ε₀A)[d − t + t/K]

C = ε₀A / (d − t + t/K)

Slab fills the gap (t = d): C = Kε₀A/d = KC₀. Metal slab (K = ∞) of thickness t: C = ε₀A/(d − t).

Example: K = 4 slab, t = d/2 → C = ε₀A/(d/2 + d/8) = 8C₀/5 = 1.6 C₀.

Q. Derive the energy stored in a capacitor. Express it in terms of field (energy density).3 M

Work to move dq against potential q/C: dW = (q/C)dq → W = ∫₀^Q q/C dq = Q²/2C.

U = Q²/2C = ½CV² = ½QV   |   u = U/(Ad) = ½ ε₀E²

Camera flash case: C = 500 μF, V = 300 V → U = ½ × 500×10⁻⁶ × 9×10⁴ = 22.5 J; released in 1 ms → P = 22.5 kW. A capacitor delivers huge power in a short burst, which a battery cannot.

Example (a): 4 μF at 100 V, K = 3 → C = 12 μF, Q = 400 μC, V = 33.3 V, U: 0.02 J → 0.0067 J, so ΔU = −0.0133 J.

Plate separation doubled, battery disconnected: Q same, C halves, V doubles, E = σ/ε₀ unchanged, U doubles.

Q. Capacitors 2 μF, 3 μF, 6 μF across 12 V in series and then in parallel. Find energy in each case.3 M · repeated

Series: 1/C = ½ + ⅓ + ⅙ = 1 → C = 1 μF, U = ½ × 1×10⁻⁶ × 144 = 72 μJ.
Parallel: C = 11 μF, U = ½ × 11×10⁻⁶ × 144 = 792 μJ. Ratio parallel : series = 11 : 1.

Q. Two identical capacitors C₁, C₂ (in parallel) are charged by a battery; the switch is opened and slabs of K are inserted in both. What happens?2–3 M

If C₁ stays connected to the battery and C₂ is isolated after the switch opens: C₁ → V same, Q₁ = K × CV. C₂ → Q same, V₂ = V/K. (Read the figure: whichever capacitor remains connected keeps V.)

Q. A charged capacitor (5 μF, 100 V) is connected to an identical uncharged capacitor. Find the common potential and energy loss.2 M

V = Q/(2C) = 50 V. Ui = ½ × 5×10⁻⁶ × 10⁴ = 0.025 J; Uf = ½ × 10×10⁻⁶ × 2500 = 0.0125 J → half the energy is lost as heat/radiation.

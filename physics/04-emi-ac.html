1. Faraday's and Lenz's laws

Q. State Faraday's laws and Lenz's law. Show that Lenz's law obeys conservation of energy.2–3 M

ε = − N dΦ/dt,   Φ = B⃗·A⃗ = BA cos θ
  1. An emf is induced whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes.
  2. Its magnitude equals the rate of change of flux linkage.

Lenz's law: the induced current flows in a direction that opposes the change in flux producing it (the minus sign).

Energy: when a magnet's N-pole approaches a coil, the near face of the coil becomes an N-pole and repels it. Work must be done against this repulsion, and that work appears as electrical energy. If the induced current aided the change, the magnet would accelerate by itself and create energy from nothing. So Lenz's law is a consequence of conservation of energy.

A–R trap: "Lenz's law violates conservation of energy" and "induced emf supports the change" are both false. Answer: D.

Q. A magnet falls through a copper ring / long solenoid. Compare its acceleration with g.1 M

Less than g. The induced current opposes the relative motion (above the ring it repels the approaching magnet, below it attracts the receding one).

Q. Flux through a loop varies with time as shown in a graph. How do you plot the induced current?3 M

ε = −A dB/dt. Constant slope → constant emf; flat part → zero emf; falling part → emf of opposite sign. E.g. r = 12 cm, R = 8.5 Ω, dB/dt = 0.5 T/s: ε = π(0.12)²×0.5 = 0.0226 V, I = 2.66 mA, then 0, then −2.66 mA.

Φ = 8t² + 5t + 7 → ε = dΦ/dt = 16t + 5 → at t = 4 s, ε = 69 V.

2. Motional emf & eddy currents

Q. Derive the emf induced in a rod of length l moving with velocity v ⟂ B on rails. Find the force needed and show that mechanical power = electrical power.3–5 M

Flux Φ = Blx. ε = −dΦ/dt = −Bl dx/dt →

|ε| = Blv,   I = Blv/R,   F = IlB = B²l²v/R

Mechanical power P = Fv = B²l²v²/R = I²R = electrical power dissipated. ✔

Example: l = 0.5 m, v = 4 m/s, B = 0.3 T, R = 0.6 Ω: ε = 0.6 V, I = 1 A, F = 0.15 N, P = 0.6 W.

Rod rotating about one end: ε = ½Bωl². (0.8 T, 20 rad/s, 0.5 m → 2 V.)

Rectangular loop (8 cm × 2 cm) leaving a field of 0.3 T at 1 cm/s: velocity ⟂ to the longer side → ε = 0.3 × 0.08 × 0.01 = 2.4×10⁻⁴ V for 2 s; ⟂ to the shorter side → 0.6×10⁻⁴ V for 8 s.

Q. What are eddy currents? Give their uses and how to reduce them.2–3 M · case study

Circulating currents induced in the bulk of a conductor when the flux through it changes. They oppose the motion (Lenz) and heat the conductor (I²R).

  • Uses: electromagnetic (eddy-current) brakes in trains, electromagnetic damping in galvanometers, induction furnace, induction cooktop, speedometers.
  • Reduce: use laminated cores (thin insulated sheets) to increase resistance of eddy current paths; slotted plates.
  • Eddy-current brakes give smooth braking without mechanical wear since there is no contact; kinetic energy → heat.

3. Self and mutual inductance

Q. Define self-inductance. Derive L for a long solenoid. How does it change if N is doubled?2–5 M · ~7 papers

Self-inductance: NΦ = LI; also ε = −L dI/dt. SI unit henry (H).

B = μ₀nI = μ₀NI/l. Flux linkage NΦ = N · BA = μ₀N²AI/l →

L = μ₀N²A / l = μ₀n²Al   (with core: μr × this)

Doubling N (same l, A) → L becomes 4L. Increase L with more turns, larger area, or an iron core.

Example: N = 500, l = 0.5 m, A = 2×10⁻⁴ m²: L = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 25×10⁴ × 2×10⁻⁴ / 0.5 = 1.26×10⁻⁴ H; current 0→5 A in 0.1 s → ε = 6.3 mV.

Energy stored: U = ½LI² (from W = ∫LI dI). E.g. 2 H at 5 A → 25 J. Current 5→1 A in 0.2 s through 2 H → ε = 40 V. 5→2 A in 0.1 s gives 50 V → L = 50 × 0.1/3 = 1.67 H.

Q. Define mutual inductance. Derive M for two coaxial solenoids / a solenoid and a coaxial loop. What affects M?3 M

N₂Φ₂ = MI₁; ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt.

Inner solenoid (radius r₁, N₂ turns) inside outer (n₁ turns/m) of length l: field of outer B = μ₀n₁I₁, flux linkage with inner = N₂ · μ₀n₁I₁ · πr₁² →

M = μ₀ n₁ n₂ π r₁² l

Small loop of radius r at the centre of a long solenoid (n turns/m): M = μ₀nπr².

M decreases when the coils are separated; increases with more turns; increases with an iron core (μr); a thin iron sheet between coils shields and reduces flux linkage. Example: primary 7 A → 3 A in 0.04 s with M = 0.5 H → ε = 50 V.

4. AC generator

Q. With a labelled diagram, explain the principle, construction and working of an AC generator. Derive the expression for instantaneous emf. When is it maximum?5 M · 11 papers

Principle: electromagnetic induction — when a coil rotates in a magnetic field, the flux through it changes continuously and an alternating emf is induced.

Construction: armature coil (N turns, area A) on a soft-iron core; strong field magnets; slip rings rotating with the coil; carbon brushes pressing on the rings to connect to the load.

Working / derivation: at time t, the normal to the coil makes angle θ = ωt with B.

Φ = NBA cos ωt  ⇒  ε = −dΦ/dt = NBAω sin ωt = ε₀ sin ωt

ε₀ = NBAω is maximum when sin ωt = ±1, i.e. the plane of the coil is parallel to B (flux zero, but changing fastest): t = T/4, 3T/4. Emf is zero when the plane is ⟂ to B (t = 0, T/2, T).

Source of energy: mechanical work done to rotate the coil against the opposing (Lenz) torque.

Examples: N = 200, A = 0.02 m², B = 0.5 T, ω = 50 rad/s → ε₀ = 100 V, εrms = 70.7 V. Coil 100 turns, 0.1 m², 60 rev/s, 0.2 T → ε₀ = 100 × 0.2 × 0.1 × 2π × 60 = 754 V. Coil r = 8 cm, 20 turns, 50 rad/s, 3×10⁻² T → ε₀ = 20 × π(0.08)² × 3×10⁻² × 50 = 0.603 V; with R = 10 Ω: I₀ = 0.0603 A, average power = ½ε₀I₀ = 0.018 W.

5. AC through R, L, C

Q. Show that in a pure inductor the current lags the voltage by π/2, and in a pure capacitor it leads by π/2. Draw graphs and phasors.3–5 M

Inductor: v = V₀ sin ωt = L di/dt → i = −(V₀/ωL) cos ωt = I₀ sin(ωt − π/2). XL = ωL.

Capacitor: q = Cv → i = dq/dt = ωCV₀ cos ωt = I₀ sin(ωt + π/2). XC = 1/ωC.

XL ∝ f (straight line through origin); XC ∝ 1/f (hyperbola). For DC (f = 0) a capacitor blocks current and an inductor offers no reactance.

Average power in pure L or C is zero: P = VrmsIrms cos(π/2) = 0 — energy stored in one quarter-cycle is returned in the next. Such current is called wattless current.

Bulb in series with a capacitor: on DC it does not glow (after charging). On AC it glows; reducing C increases XC → dimmer. Bulb with an iron-cored inductor: removing the iron reduces L, lowers XL → brighter. Adding a capacitor with XC = XL → resonance → brightest.

6. Series LCR, resonance, power

Q. Using a phasor diagram, derive the impedance of a series LCR circuit and the phase angle.5 M · very frequent

V² = VR² + (VL − VC)² = I²[R² + (XL − XC)²]

Z = √(R² + (XL − XC)²),   tan φ = (XL − XC)/R

XL > XC: inductive, current lags. XL < XC: capacitive, current leads.

Example: R = 30 Ω, XL = 70 Ω, XC = 30 Ω, 200 V: Z = 50 Ω, I = 4 A, tan φ = 4/3 → φ = 53°, current lags.

Why can VR + VL + VC exceed the source voltage? They are not in phase, so they add as phasors, not algebraically.

Q. What is resonance? Derive the resonant frequency. Plot I vs ω. Define Q-factor. How is it used in radio tuning?3–5 M

At resonance XL = XC → ω₀L = 1/ω₀C →

ω₀ = 1/√(LC),   f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)),   Zmin = R,   Imax = V/R

Quality factor Q = ω₀L/R = (1/R)√(L/C) = ω₀/(bandwidth). A high Q (small R) gives a sharp peak and better selectivity.

Radio tuning: C is varied so the circuit's f₀ equals the desired station's frequency; that station's current is maximum while others are suppressed. A sharper curve (higher Q) avoids adjacent-channel interference.

Examples: L = 2 H, C = 32 μF: ω₀ = 1/√(64×10⁻⁶) = 125 rad/s. L = 0.1 H, C = 10 μF, R = 10 Ω, 100 V: ω₀ = 1000 rad/s, I = 10 A. R = 10, L = 0.5 H, C = 20 μF: ω₀ = 316 rad/s, Q = 15.8.

Q. Derive the average power in a series LCR circuit. Define power factor. When is power maximum? When is current wattless?3–5 M

v = V₀ sin ωt, i = I₀ sin(ωt − φ). p = vi = V₀I₀ sin ωt sin(ωt − φ) = (V₀I₀/2)[cos φ − cos(2ωt − φ)]. Average of cos(2ωt − φ) over a cycle is 0:

Pavg = Vrms Irms cos φ,   power factor cos φ = R/Z
  • Maximum power: cos φ = 1, φ = 0 → purely resistive or at resonance.
  • Zero power (wattless current): φ = ±90° → pure L or pure C (R = 0).

Example: 80 mH and 250 μF in series, 240 V, 100 rad/s, R ≈ 0: XL = 8 Ω, XC = 40 Ω, Z = 32 Ω, I = 7.5 A, average power = 0.

Choke coil: preferred over a resistor to limit AC current because an ideal inductor consumes no average power.

7. Transformer

Q. With a labelled diagram, explain the principle and working of a step-up transformer. Derive the voltage and current ratios. State the energy losses.5 M · 9 papers

Principle: mutual induction. AC in the primary sets up an alternating flux in the core, which links the secondary and induces an emf in it.

Derivation (ideal: same flux links both coils, negligible resistance, no losses):

εp = −Np dΦ/dt, εs = −Ns dΦ/dt →

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np = Ip/Is  (since VpIp = VsIs)

Step-up: Ns > Np → voltage up, current down by the same factor; power is unchanged, so it does not violate energy conservation and cannot increase power.

Energy losses and remedies:

  • Flux leakage — wind coils over each other.
  • Copper (I²R) loss — thick copper wire.
  • Eddy current loss — laminated core.
  • Hysteresis loss — soft iron with a narrow loop.

Why not DC? Steady DC produces constant flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and no emf is induced in the secondary.

Why step-up for transmission? High voltage → low current → low I²R loss over long distances.

Examples: 220 V → 11 kV: Ns/Np = 50. 200 turns/4000 turns, 220 V → 4400 V, Ip:Is = 20:1. Refrigerator 110 V, 550 W on 220 V supply → Ip = 2.5 A. 1150/230 turns, 200 V → 40 V; Is = 2 A → Ip = 0.4 A; 75% efficiency → output 60 W.

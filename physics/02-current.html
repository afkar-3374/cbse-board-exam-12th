1. Drift velocity, mobility, resistivity

Q. Define drift velocity and relaxation time. Derive I = neAvd.3–5 M · ~9 papers

Drift velocity vd: the average velocity with which free electrons drift opposite to the applied field. Relaxation time τ: average time between successive collisions.

Each electron accelerates a = −eE/m. Averaging v = u + at over all electrons (average u = 0, average t = τ):

vd = − eEτ / m

Charge crossing area A in Δt: Q = (n · A · vdΔt) e →

I = neAvd   |   j = I/A = nevd

Substituting vd: j = (ne²τ/m) E = σE → σ = ne²τ/m, ρ = m/(ne²τ). This is Ohm's law in microscopic form, and shows current density is along E.

Example: I = 1.6 A, A = 10⁻⁶ m², n = 8×10²⁸ → vd = 1.6/(8×10²⁸ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 10⁻⁶) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.

Q. If drift speed is only mm/s, why does a bulb glow instantly?2 M · A–R

The electric field is set up throughout the circuit almost at the speed of light, so electrons everywhere in the wire start drifting at once; electrons do not have to travel from the switch to the bulb. (A–R tip: "n is very high (~10²⁸ m⁻³)" is true but is not the explanation.)

Q. Define mobility. Does it change if the potential difference is doubled?2 M

μ = vd/E = eτ/m   (SI: m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹)

Doubling V doubles E and vd, but μ = eτ/m is unchanged (it depends only on the material and temperature).

Q. Ratio of drift velocities in two wires of the same material (a) in series, (b) in parallel.2–3 M · repeated

Series (same I): vd = I/(neA) → v₁/v₂ = A₂/A₁ = (r₂/r₁)². Radii 3 : 2 → v₁ : v₂ = 4 : 9.

Parallel (same V): vd = eEτ/m = eVτ/(mL) → v ∝ 1/L, independent of area. Lengths L and 2L → 2 : 1.

Different materials in series with nX = 2nY, same area → vX/vY = nY/nX = 1 : 2.

A tapering wire: current is the same everywhere (charge conservation), but vd is larger at the thinner end.

2. Temperature dependence

Q. Plot resistivity vs temperature for copper, nichrome and a semiconductor. Explain using ρ = m/(ne²τ).3–5 M

  • Metals: n is nearly constant; with rising T, ions vibrate more, collisions are more frequent → τ decreases → ρ increases. ρT = ρ₀[1 + α(T − T₀)], α positive.
  • Nichrome / manganin / constantan: very small α → used in standard resistors.
  • Semiconductors: n increases rapidly with T (outweighs the fall in τ) → ρ decreases; α negative (Si, Ge, carbon).

Example: 20% increase from 27 °C with α = 2×10⁻⁴ °C⁻¹: 0.2 = αΔT → ΔT = 1000 °C → T = 1027 °C.

3. EMF, internal resistance, cell combinations

Q. Distinguish EMF and terminal voltage. What is the terminal voltage while (a) discharging, (b) charging?2 M

Discharging: V = E − Ir   |   Charging: V = E + Ir

I = E/(R + r). V = E only when no current flows (open circuit, R → ∞). Max current = E/r (short circuit).

Example (charging): 8 V, r = 0.5 Ω, charged from 120 V through 15.5 Ω: I = (120 − 8)/16 = 7 A → V = 8 + 7×0.5 = 11.5 V.

Internal resistance from two readings: E = I₁(R₁ + r) = I₂(R₂ + r) → r = (I₂R₂ − I₁R₁)/(I₁ − I₂).

Power to load is maximum when R = r: Pmax = E²/4r. Efficiency = R/(R + r).

Q. Derive the equivalent EMF and internal resistance of two cells in parallel.3 M

V = E₁ − I₁r₁ = E₂ − I₂r₂, and I = I₁ + I₂ = (E₁ − V)/r₁ + (E₂ − V)/r₂.

Solving: V = (E₁r₂ + E₂r₁)/(r₁ + r₂) − I · r₁r₂/(r₁ + r₂). Comparing with V = Eeq − I req:

Eeq = (E₁r₂ + E₂r₁)/(r₁ + r₂),    req = r₁r₂/(r₁ + r₂)

Series: Eeq = E₁ + E₂, req = r₁ + r₂.

Example: 1.5 V (0.2 Ω) ∥ 2.0 V (0.3 Ω): E = (0.45 + 0.4)/0.5 = 1.7 V, r = 0.06/0.5 = 0.12 Ω.

Two identical cells (E, r) — same current through R in series and parallel when R = r.

4. Kirchhoff's rules & Wheatstone bridge

Q. State Kirchhoff's rules. On which conservation laws are they based?2 M

  1. Junction rule: ΣI at any junction = 0 (current in = current out) → conservation of charge.
  2. Loop rule: algebraic sum of changes in potential around any closed loop = 0 (ΣE = ΣIR) → conservation of energy.

Sign convention: going through a resistor along the current → −IR; going from − to + terminal of a cell → +E.

Q. Using Kirchhoff's rules, derive the balance condition of a Wheatstone bridge.3 M · ~8 papers

Loop ABDA: −I₁P − IgG + I₂R = 0. Loop BCDB: −(I₁ − Ig)Q + (I₂ + Ig)S + IgG = 0.

At balance Ig = 0: I₁P = I₂R and I₁Q = I₂S. Dividing:

P/Q = R/S

Meter bridge: R/S = l/(100 − l). Example: null at 40 cm, then 10 Ω added in series with R₁ gives null at 60 cm: R₁/R₂ = 2/3 and (R₁ + 10)/R₂ = 3/2 → R₂ = 12 Ω, R₁ = 8 Ω.

Q. Why should a voltmeter have very high resistance and an ammeter very low resistance?2 M

A voltmeter is connected in parallel; if its resistance were low it would draw current and lower the very p.d. it measures. An ammeter is in series; a high resistance would reduce the current it measures. Ideal: RV = ∞, RA = 0.

Q. A 12 Ω wire is stretched to twice its length and bent into a circle. Find the resistance between diametrically opposite points.2 M

Stretching to 2L (volume constant): R' = n²R = 4 × 12 = 48 Ω. Two halves of 24 Ω in parallel → 12 Ω.

Remember: same material, Q has double diameter and half length → RQ = R × (½)/(4) = R/8.

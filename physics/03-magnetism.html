1. Force on a moving charge

Q. A charged particle enters a uniform B field. Discuss its path when v ⟂ B, v ∥ B, and at an angle θ.2–4 M · case study

F⃗ = q(v⃗ × B⃗)  ⇒  F = qvB sin θ
  • v ∥ B: F = 0 → straight line.
  • v ⟂ B: circle. qvB = mv²/r → r = mv/qB = p/qB = √(2mK)/qB. Period T = 2πm/qB, frequency ν = qB/2πm — both independent of speed.
  • At angle θ: helix. Radius r = mv sin θ/qB, pitch = v cos θ · T = 2πmv cos θ/qB.

The magnetic force is always ⟂ to v, so it does no work: speed and kinetic energy stay constant, only direction (velocity) changes.

Proton vs α-particle (mα = 4m, qα = 2e):

  • Same velocity: r ∝ m/q → rp : rα = 1 : 2.
  • Same KE: r ∝ √m/q → rp : rα = 1 : 1.
  • Same accelerating potential: r ∝ √(m/q) → 1 : √2.

Q. Velocity selector: show that particles with v = E/B pass undeflected.case study

With E ⟂ B ⟂ v, electric force qE and magnetic force qvB act in opposite directions. Undeflected when qE = qvB → v = E/B (independent of q and m). E = 3×10⁴ V/m, B = 0.2 T → v = 1.5 × 10⁵ m/s. In a mass spectrometer, the second region then gives r = mv/qB′ ∝ m.

2. Biot–Savart law: circular loop

Q. State the Biot–Savart law. Derive B on the axis of a circular loop; hence find B at its centre.3–5 M · ~6 papers

dB⃗ = (μ₀/4π) · I (dl⃗ × r⃗) / r³    dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl sin θ / r²
  1. dl ⟂ r, so dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl/(x² + R²).
  2. Axial component dBx = dB · R/√(x² + R²).
  3. Integrate ∮dl = 2πR:
B = μ₀ I R² / [2(x² + R²)^{3/2}]  (N turns: × N)

At the centre (x = 0): B = μ₀NI/2R. Far away (x ≫ R): B = μ₀IR²/2x³ = (μ₀/4π)·2m/x³ (a magnetic dipole, m = IπR²).

Field at x = R: B0/2√2. Field equals B0/8 at x = √3 R. Same wire bent into 2 turns: B → 4B (N doubles, R halves).

3. Ampere's circuital law

Q. State Ampere's circuital law. Find B (a) due to a long straight wire, (b) inside and outside a thick wire of radius a, (c) inside a long solenoid.2–5 M

∮ B⃗ · dl⃗ = μ₀ Ienclosed

(a) Circle of radius r around the wire: B·2πr = μ₀I → B = μ₀I/2πr (so at 2r the field is B/2, not B/4).

(b) Outside (r > a): B = μ₀I/2πr. Inside (r < a): Ienc = I r²/a² → B = μ₀Ir/2πa² (linear, max at surface).

Ratio of B at a/2 above surface (r = 3a/2) to a/2 below (r = a/2): (1/(3a/2)) / ((a/2)/a²) = (2/3)/(1/2) = 4 : 3.

(c) Rectangular Amperian loop with one side inside: B·L = μ₀nLI → B = μ₀nI (with core: μ₀μrnI). Independent of the solenoid's radius. Outside ≈ 0 because field lines spread over a huge area.

Example: 1000 turns/m, 5 A → B = 4π×10⁻⁷ × 1000 × 5 = 6.28 × 10⁻³ T.

4. Force between parallel wires

Q. Derive the force per unit length between two long parallel current-carrying conductors. Define 1 ampere.3–5 M · ~6 papers

Field of wire 1 at wire 2: B₁ = μ₀I₁/2πd (into the page by the right-hand rule).

Force on length L of wire 2: F = I₂LB₁ →

F/L = μ₀ I₁ I₂ / 2πd

Force on wire 1 due to 2 is equal and opposite → obeys Newton's third law.

1 ampere is the current which, flowing in two infinitely long parallel straight conductors 1 m apart in vacuum, produces a force of 2 × 10⁻⁷ N per metre of length between them.

Example: 5 A and 10 A, 20 cm apart: F/L = 2×10⁻⁷ × 50/0.2 = 5 × 10⁻⁵ N/m (attractive). Midpoint field: μ₀(10 − 5)/(2π × 0.1) = 10⁻⁵ T.

5. Torque on a loop & magnetic moment

Q. Derive the torque on a rectangular current loop in a uniform field. Define magnetic dipole moment.3 M

Forces on the two sides parallel to the axis of rotation: F = IbB each, equal and opposite, separated by a sin θ → couple.

τ = NIAB sin θ  ⇒  τ⃗ = m⃗ × B⃗,   m = NIA

Net force = 0 in a uniform field (A–R favourite), but torque ≠ 0 in general. Magnetic moment m = NIA, direction by right-hand thumb rule, SI unit A m².

Plane of coil parallel to B (θ = 90°): τmax = NIAB. E.g. 100 × 2 × 0.05 × 0.4 = 4 N m.

Wire of length L made into one circular turn: r = L/2π, A = L²/4π → τmax = IL²B/4π.

Potential energy U = −m⃗·B⃗. Work to turn from stable (0°) to unstable (180°): 2mB. E.g. m = 1.5 J/T, B = 0.22 T: to 90° → 0.33 J; to 180° → 0.66 J.

Q. Show that the magnetic moment of a revolving electron is μ = −(e/2m) L.3 M

Current I = e/T = ev/2πr. μ = IA = (ev/2πr)·πr² = evr/2. Angular momentum L = mvr → μ = (e/2m)L. Since the electron is negative, μ⃗ is opposite to L⃗: μ⃗ = −(e/2m)L⃗.

6. Moving coil galvanometer

Q. Explain the principle and working of a moving coil galvanometer. Why is the field radial? Why a soft-iron core?3–5 M · case study

Principle: a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a torque. Deflecting torque NIAB is balanced by the spring's restoring torque kφ:

NIAB = kφ  ⇒  φ = (NAB/k) I
  • Radial field (concave pole pieces): plane of the coil is always parallel to B, so sin θ = 1 and φ ∝ I → linear scale.
  • Soft-iron core: increases B (stronger, radial), increasing sensitivity.
  • Coil on a metal frame: eddy currents provide electromagnetic damping, so the pointer settles quickly.

Current sensitivity φ/I = NAB/k. Voltage sensitivity φ/V = NAB/(kG). Doubling N doubles current sensitivity but also doubles G, so voltage sensitivity is unchanged.

Q. How is a galvanometer converted into (a) an ammeter, (b) a voltmeter?2–3 M · ~10 papers

Ammeter: S = IgG / (I − Ig)   |   Voltmeter: R = V/Ig − G

Examples:

  • G = 100 Ω, Ig = 1 mA → 0–1 A ammeter: S = 0.1/0.999 ≈ 0.1 Ω.
  • G = 15 Ω, Ig = 30 mA → 0–15 V voltmeter: R = 500 − 15 = 485 Ω in series.
  • G = 50 Ω, Ig = 5 mA → 0–5 V: R = 1000 − 50 = 950 Ω.
  • G = 6 Ω, Ig = 0.2 A → 0–5 A: S = 1.2/4.8 = 0.25 Ω; ammeter resistance = 6×0.25/6.25 = 0.24 Ω.
  • Voltmeter range V with R₁, 2V with R₂: G = R₂ − 2R₁.

Why series for a voltmeter? A large series resistance means it draws negligible current and does not disturb the circuit; in parallel it would act as a shunt.

Curie's law example: χ = 2.4×10⁻⁵ at 300 K → at 600 K, χ = 1.2×10⁻⁵. Magnetisation M ∝ B/T: 6×10⁻³ A/m at (0.6 T, 300 K) → at (0.2 T, 600 K): 6×10⁻³ × (1/3)(1/2) = 1×10⁻³ A/m.

Q. Which material is used for (a) permanent magnets, (b) transformer cores / electromagnets? Why?1–2 M

(a) Permanent magnets: high retentivity and high coercivity (steel, alnico). (b) Cores: high permeability, low retentivity, low coercivity and a narrow hysteresis loop (soft iron) — less energy lost per cycle, easy to magnetise/demagnetise.

Q. Gauss's law in magnetism — what does it imply? Compare a bar magnet and a solenoid.1–2 M

∮B⃗·dA⃗ = 0 → isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist; magnetic field lines form closed loops. A bar magnet and a current-carrying solenoid have identical external field patterns (the solenoid is an "artificial bar magnet"). A bar magnet cut across its length: each half has half the pole-length, same pole strength, half the magnetic moment.

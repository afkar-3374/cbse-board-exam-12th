1. Electromagnetic waves

Q. What is displacement current? Why was it needed?2 M

Id = ε₀ dΦE/dt   |   ∮B⃗·dl⃗ = μ₀(Ic + Id)

For a charging capacitor, Ampere's law gives different answers for a surface through the wire (I) and a surface passing between the plates (0) — an inconsistency. Maxwell added the displacement current, produced by the changing electric field between the plates. It equals the conduction current in the wires (C = 20 μF, dV/dt = 3 V/s → Ic = Id = 60 μA). It can exist in empty space where no charges flow.

Q. Write the characteristics of EM waves. Draw an EM wave travelling along z.2–3 M

  • Produced by accelerated (oscillating) charges; a charge at constant velocity does not radiate.
  • Transverse: E⃗ ⟂ B⃗ ⟂ direction of propagation (E⃗ × B⃗ gives direction); E and B are in phase.
  • Speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = E₀/B₀ in vacuum; v = c/n in a medium.
  • Need no medium; carry energy and momentum.

Examples: E₀ = 60 V/m → B₀ = 2 × 10⁻⁷ T. E along +y, travelling along +x → B along +z. E along +z, travelling +x → B along −y. B = 4×10⁻⁸ sin(6π×10⁸t + 2πx) → travels along −x (signs of ωt and kx same).

From B = B₀ sin(kx − ωt): λ = 2π/k, f = ω/2π. With k = 1.5π×10⁷: λ = 1.33×10⁻⁷ m (UV). In a medium the wave speed is v = ω/k = E₀/B₀ and the refractive index is n = c/v.

2. Mirrors & refraction basics

Q. Derive the mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f for a concave mirror.3 M

Triangles A′B′P and ABP (similar): A′B′/AB = B′P/BP. Triangles A′B′F and MPF (MP = AB, for small aperture): A′B′/AB = B′F/FP.

So B′P/BP = B′F/FP = (B′P − FP)/FP. With sign convention B′P = −v, BP = −u, FP = −f: (−v)/(−u) = (−v + f)/(−f) → vf = uv − uf → divide by uvf:

1/v + 1/u = 1/f,   m = −v/u

Example: h = 2 cm at u = −30 cm, f = −20 cm: v = −60 cm, m = −2, image 4 cm, real and inverted. Mirror image n times, real: u = −(n + 1)f/n.

A ray along the principal axis retraces its path because it hits the mirror normally (along the radius).

Q. Apparent depth and speed of light: a tank 30 cm deep appears 16 cm deep.1 M

n = real/apparent = 30/16 = 1.875 → v = c/n = 1.6 × 10⁸ m/s. Wavelength in a medium: λm = λ/n (600 → 400 nm gives n = 1.5); frequency does not change on refraction (it is fixed by the source). Sodium light 5893 Å in water (n = 1.33): λ = 4431 Å, v = 2.26×10⁸ m/s, f = 5.09×10¹⁴ Hz.

3. Total internal reflection & optical fibre

Q. Define critical angle and TIR. Derive n = 1/sin C. State the conditions for TIR.3–5 M · case study in 7 papers

Critical angle C: the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is 90°. Snell's law n sin C = 1 · sin 90° →

n = 1 / sin C  (for denser medium relative to rarer: rnd = 1/sin C)

Conditions: (1) light travels from denser to rarer; (2) angle of incidence > C.

Optical fibre: core of higher n surrounded by cladding of lower n. Light entering at a suitable angle strikes the core–cladding boundary at i > C and undergoes repeated TIR, so it travels long distances with negligible loss even when bent. Cladding must have lower n, otherwise TIR is impossible. Uses: communication, endoscopy.

Examples: n = 1.5 → C = sin⁻¹(2/3) ≈ 42° (TIR at 50°, not at 30° or 40°). Core 1.5, cladding 1.2 → C = sin⁻¹(0.8); a ray at 60° (sin 60 = 0.87 > 0.8) is totally reflected. Core 1.50, cladding 1.40: nrel = 1.07, C = sin⁻¹(14/15). C = 45° → n = √2. C = 30° → n = 2, v = 1.5×10⁸ m/s. Diamond C = 24.4° → n = 2.42 (sparkle from multiple TIR). Water n = 4/3 → C = sin⁻¹(3/4) ≈ 48.6°.

Looming / mirage: TIR in air layers of varying density; the image is virtual.

4. Refraction at a spherical surface & lens maker's formula

Q. Derive the relation n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R for refraction at a convex spherical surface. State the assumptions.3–5 M

Assumptions: point object on the principal axis; small aperture; paraxial rays (angles small, tan θ ≈ sin θ ≈ θ); M close to the pole.

From the triangles: i = α + γ, r = γ − β. With tan α ≈ MN/OM, tan γ ≈ MN/MC, tan β ≈ MN/MI.

Snell (small angles): n₁ i = n₂ r → n₁(MN/OM + MN/MC) = n₂(MN/MC − MN/MI).

Using OM = −u, MI = +v, MC = +R:

n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R

Example: glass sphere R = 6 cm, n = 1.5, air bubble 3 cm from centre (u = −3 cm from the near surface, light going glass → air): n₁ = 1.5, n₂ = 1, R = −6 cm (centre on the incoming side): 1/v + 1.5/3 = (−0.5)/(−6) → 1/v = 1/12 − 1/2 → v = −2.4 cm. The bubble appears 2.4 cm inside the surface (3.6 cm from the centre).

Q. Derive the lens maker's formula for a thin double convex lens.3–5 M · 9 papers

Surface 1 (n₁ → n₂): n₂/v₁ − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R₁ … (1)

Surface 2 (n₂ → n₁), object at I₁ (thin lens): n₁/v − n₂/v₁ = (n₁ − n₂)/R₂ … (2)

Adding: n₁(1/v − 1/u) = (n₂ − n₁)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). For u = ∞, v = f:

1/f = (n₂/n₁ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)   and   1/v − 1/u = 1/f

In air with refractive index n: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). Equiconvex (R₁ = R, R₂ = −R): f = R/2(n − 1); for n = 1.5, f = R. Plano-convex: 1/f = (n − 1)/R.

Q. A lens (n = 1.5) is placed in a liquid. Find the new focal length / nature. (~12 papers)2–3 M

fliquid/fair = (n − 1) / (n/nl − 1)
  • Water (4/3): (0.5)/(1.125 − 1) = 4 → f = 4 × 20 = 80 cm.
  • nl = 1.25: ratio 0.5/0.2 = 2.5 → 20 cm lens becomes 50 cm.
  • nl = 1.5 (same as lens): 1/f = 0 → f = ∞, behaves like a plane glass sheet.
  • nl = 1.6 or 1.65 > n: f negative → convex lens becomes diverging. f = 20 cm in 1.65: (0.5)/(1.5/1.65 − 1) = −5.5 → f = −110 cm. A concave lens in a denser liquid becomes converging.
  • Glass lens of +5 D acts as diverging lens of f = −100 cm in liquid n: (0.5)/(1.5/n − 1) = −100/20 → n = 5/3.

A lens becomes less powerful as the liquid's n approaches the lens's n. Blue light: n higher → f shorter than for red (chromatic aberration). nv = 1.55, nr = 1.50, fv = 20 cm → fr = 20 × 0.55/0.50 = 22 cm.

Q. Power and combination of lenses.1–2 M

P = 1/f (m) dioptre;   P = P₁ + P₂,   1/F = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂

+10 cm and +15 cm in contact: F = 6 cm, P = 16.7 D. +20 cm and −15 cm: P = 5 − 6.67 = −1.67 D (diverging). +5 D and −2 D: f = 33.3 cm. +40 and −20: F = −40 cm → image of an object at infinity is 40 cm in front (virtual). Convex f₁ with concave f₂ (f₁ > f₂): P = (f₂ − f₁)/(f₁f₂), diverging.

An equiconvex lens of power P cut perpendicular to the axis → each half has P/2; recombined → P. Covering half a lens: full image, reduced intensity.

Plano-convex lens, light enters the curved face, plane face silvered: behaves as a concave mirror with f = R/2(n − 1). n = 1.5, R = 10 cm → f = 10 cm. If instead the curved face is silvered: f = R/2n.

Q. Displacement method: screen fixed at distance D from object; two lens positions separated by d give sharp images.case study

f = (D² − d²)/4D,   d = √(D(D − 4f)),   requires D ≥ 4f

D = 80 cm, d = 20 cm → f = 18.75 cm. First position gives an enlarged image, the second a diminished image. For f = 15 cm, D must exceed 60 cm (65 cm works).

5. Prism

Q. Trace a ray through a prism. Show i + e = A + δ and derive n = sin((A + δm)/2)/sin(A/2). Plot δ vs i.3–5 M · 11 papers

In the quadrilateral formed by the two normals: r₁ + r₂ = A. Total deviation δ = (i − r₁) + (e − r₂) = i + e − A → i + e = A + δ.

At minimum deviation the ray passes symmetrically: i = e, r₁ = r₂ = A/2, so i = (A + δm)/2. Snell: n = sin i / sin r₁ →

n = sin[(A + δm)/2] / sin(A/2)

Examples: A = 60°, δm = 30° → n = sin 45°/sin 30° = √2 (in 4 papers!). Then v = c/√2 = 2.12×10⁸ m/s. A = 60°, n = 1.5 → sin((60 + δ)/2) = 0.75 → (60 + δ)/2 = 49° → δm = 38°. n = √2, A = 60° → δm = 30°, i = 45°. If i = (3/4)A = 45° at minimum deviation → δm = 30°, n = √2. If δm = A: n = 2 cos(A/2).

Thin prism: δ = (n − 1)A. Ray emerging normally from the second face: r₂ = 0, r₁ = A → i ≈ nA.

Grazing emergence / just TIR at the second face: r₂ = C → r₁ = A − C. For A = 60°, n = √2: C = 45°, r₁ = 15°, sin i = √2 sin 15° → i ≈ 21.5°.

Dispersion: violet deviates more than red because nviolet > nred (shorter wavelength).

Right-angled isosceles prism (45°): TIR at the hypotenuse if n > √2 = 1.414. Rays with n = 1.39 refract out; 1.47 and 1.52 are totally reflected.

6. Microscope & telescope

Q. Draw a labelled ray diagram of a compound microscope. Derive its magnifying power (a) at the near point, (b) at infinity.5 M · 9 papers

M = m₀ × mₑ,   m₀ = v₀/|u₀| ≈ L/f₀

(a) Final image at D: mₑ = 1 + D/fₑ → M = (L/f₀)(1 + D/fₑ).

(b) Final image at infinity (normal adjustment): mₑ = D/fₑ → M = (L/f₀)(D/fₑ).

Both f₀ and fₑ should be small (f₀ < fₑ); the objective has a short focal length and small aperture to bring the object close and form a large real image.

Examples: f₀ = 1 cm, fₑ = 5 cm, L = 20 cm, image at infinity: M = 20 × 5 = 100. f₀ = 1, fₑ = 2, L = 20, near point: M = 20 × 13.5 = 270. Combination P (1 cm, 5 cm) beats Q (2 cm, 10 cm) by a factor of 4.

Simple microscope: m = 1 + D/f at D; m = D/f at infinity. f = 15 cm, image at 25 cm: u = −9.4 cm, m = 2.67; at infinity m = 1.67.

Q. Draw a ray diagram of a refracting astronomical telescope in normal adjustment and derive its magnifying power. Why is the objective large? Advantages of a reflecting telescope?5 M · ~13 papers

M = β/α. α ≈ h/f₀ (angle at objective), β ≈ h/fₑ (angle at eyepiece) →

M = f₀/fₑ (normal adjustment),   L = f₀ + fₑ

Final image at D: M = (f₀/fₑ)(1 + fₑ/D).

Objective: large focal length (high M) and large aperture (gathers more light, better resolving power). Choose the lens with largest f and aperture as objective, smallest f as eyepiece.

Reflecting telescope (Cassegrain) advantages: no chromatic aberration (mirror), spherical aberration removed with a parabolic mirror, a mirror is lighter/easier to support than a huge lens, cheaper to make large → more light and higher resolution.

Examples: f₀ = 100 cm, fₑ = 5 cm → M = 20, L = 105 cm. f₀ = 120, fₑ = 6 → 20. A (100, 5): M = 20, L = 105; B (80, 10): M = 8, L = 90. f₀ = 50fₑ with L = 102 cm → fₑ = 2 cm, f₀ = 100 cm.

7. Wavefronts & Huygens' principle

Q. What is a wavefront? State Huygens' principle. Draw the wavefronts for a point source, a distant source, after a prism, convex lens, concave mirror.2 M

Wavefront: locus of points oscillating in the same phase. Rays are ⟂ to wavefronts.

Huygens' principle: (1) every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets spreading forward with the speed of the wave; (2) the new wavefront is the forward envelope (common tangent) of these wavelets.

  • Point source → spherical (diverging). Source at infinity / distant star / small part of a large spherical wavefront → plane.
  • Lamp at focus of concave mirror → reflected plane wavefront. Plane wavefront on concave mirror (radius R) → converging spherical of radius R/2.
  • Plane wavefront through a prism → plane but tilted (lower part, which travels through thicker glass, is delayed). Through a convex lens → converging spherical; reflected from a convex mirror → diverging spherical.

Huygens' theory explains reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction, but not the photoelectric effect or the origin of spectra.

Q. Using Huygens' principle, prove the laws of refraction (Snell's law).3–5 M · 9 papers

In ΔABC: sin i = BC/AC = v₁t/AC. In ΔAEC: sin r = AE/AC = v₂t/AC.

sin i / sin r = v₁/v₂ = n₂/n₁ = constant (Snell's law)

Incident ray, normal and refracted ray lie in one plane (second law). If v₂ < v₁ the ray bends towards the normal. Frequency stays the same; wavelength scales with speed: λ₂ = λ₁v₂/v₁.

Reflection: same construction with the wavelet in the same medium: AE = BC = vt, triangles ABC and AEC are congruent → i = r.

Example: 60° from air into glass n = 1.5, t = 2×10⁻¹⁰ s: BC = ct = 6 cm; AE = (c/1.5)t = 4 cm.

8. Interference – Young's double slit

Q. What are coherent sources? Why can't two independent sodium lamps produce sustained interference? How are coherent sources obtained in YDSE?2 M

Coherent sources emit waves of the same frequency with a constant (time-independent) phase difference. Independent lamps emit random wave-trains whose phase changes ~10⁻⁸ s, so the pattern shifts too fast to see. In YDSE both slits are illuminated by the same single slit (division of wavefront), so their phase difference is fixed. Conditions for sustained interference: coherent sources, same/similar amplitude, narrow slits close together, monochromatic light.

Q. Derive the resultant intensity of two coherent waves; hence conditions for constructive and destructive interference.3 M

y₁ = a cos ωt, y₂ = a cos(ωt + φ) → y = 2a cos(φ/2) cos(ωt + φ/2). Amplitude A = 2a cos(φ/2):

I = 4I₀ cos²(φ/2)   |   I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cos φ
  • Constructive: φ = 2nπ, path difference Δ = nλ → I = 4I₀.
  • Destructive: φ = (2n − 1)π, Δ = (2n − 1)λ/2 → I = 0.

Intensity varies between 0 and 4a². Path diff λ/4 → φ = π/2 → I = 2I₀; λ/3 → φ = 2π/3 → I = I₀ (so Q is not dark). I and 4I gives resultant 5I where cos φ = 0 → φ = π/2. Energy is conserved — it is only redistributed ("light + light = darkness" at minima).

Q. Derive the fringe width in YDSE.3–5 M · ~18 papers (with numericals)

Path difference at P: S₂P − S₁P ≈ d sin θ ≈ d tan θ = yd/D.

  • Bright: yd/D = nλ → yn = nλD/d.
  • Dark: yd/D = (2n − 1)λ/2 → yn = (2n − 1)λD/2d.
β = yn+1 − yn = λD/d   (same for bright and dark fringes)

All fringes are equally wide (for small θ). β ∝ λ, β ∝ D, β ∝ 1/d. In a medium of n: β/n. Angular fringe width = λ/d.

White light: central fringe is white (Δ = 0 for all λ); next fringes are coloured — violet/blue nearest the centre, red farthest; after a few fringes, uniform illumination. Fringe width order: red > orange > green.

Examples:

  • λ = 500 nm, d = 1 mm, D = 1 m → β = 0.5 mm.
  • 600 nm, 1.2 m, 0.2 mm → β = 3.6 mm (A–R: assertion true, reason "β ∝ d" false → C).
  • d doubled and D halved → β/4. d halved, D doubled → 4β.
  • β = 0.4 mm; d × 2, D × ½ → 0.1 mm.
  • 4th bright at 1.2 cm with d = 0.28 mm, D = 1.4 m → λ = 1.2×10⁻² × 0.28×10⁻³/(4 × 1.4) = 600 nm. (If 4th dark: use 3.5β → λ ≈ 686 nm.)
  • Two wavelengths coincide: n₁λ₁ = n₂λ₂. 500 & 600 nm → 6×500 = 5×600 = 3000 nm → y = 3000 nm × D/d. (D = 1.8 m, d = 0.3 mm → 18 mm.) 650 & 520 nm → 4×650 = 5×520 → y = 2600 nm × 1.2/2×10⁻³ = 1.56 mm.
  • Bright fringe at 6 mm with β = 3 mm (1.5 m, 0.3 mm, 600 nm) → 2nd order.
  • Slits infinitely close (d → 0): β → ∞, no visible pattern.

Replacing water by a denser liquid: v and λ decrease, frequency unchanged; β decreases; fringes remain straight; central maximum does not shift.

9. Single-slit diffraction

Q. Explain single-slit diffraction. Show that the central maximum is twice as wide as the others. Sketch the intensity pattern.3 M · ~11 papers

Divide the slit (width a) into two halves. At angle θ with a sin θ = λ, each point in the upper half has a partner in the lower half with path difference λ/2 → they cancel → first minimum.

Minima: a sin θ = nλ    Secondary maxima: a sin θ ≈ (n + ½)λ

Angular width of central maximum = 2λ/a; linear width = 2λD/a, which is twice the width of the secondary fringes (λD/a). Intensity of secondary maxima falls because a smaller and smaller fraction of the slit contributes.

Effects: narrower slit → wider central maximum (and dimmer); red → blue: bands narrower and more crowded; immersing in water → width ÷ n. Diffraction is noticeable when the slit width is comparable to λ. Pinhole widened → pattern shrinks but intensity increases.

Examples:

  • λ = 600 nm, a = 0.3 mm → angular width 2λ/a = 4×10⁻³ rad.
  • λ = 600 nm, a = 0.2 mm, D = 1.5 m → width = 9 mm; with a = 0.1 mm → 18 mm.
  • λ = 500 nm, a = 0.2 mm, D = 2 m → distance between first minima = 10⁻² m.
  • a = 0.1 mm, λ = 6000 Å, D = 0.5 m → 3rd dark band at 3λD/a = 9 mm.
  • First minimum at 2.5 mm, λ = 500 nm, D = 1 m → a = 0.2 mm; first secondary max at 1.5λD/a = 3.75 mm.

Diffraction can be treated as interference of waves from many point sources on the same wavefront. Colours of thin oil films are due to interference.

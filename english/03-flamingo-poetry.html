1. My Mother at Sixty-six — Kamala Das

Q. What is the poet's "familiar ache"? What contrast does she notice?extract · repeated

Driving to Cochin airport, the poet sees her mother dozing, her face "ashen like that of a corpse" — and feels the old childhood fear of losing her. Outside she sees "young trees sprinting" and "merry children spilling out of their homes" — youth and life contrasted with her mother's decay.

Q. What is the significance of the smile at the end?2 M

At the security check she says "see you soon, Amma" and smiles and smiles (repetition) to hide her fear and pain, to reassure her mother and herself — a brave mask over deep anxiety.

2. Keeping Quiet — Pablo Neruda

Q. What does the poet want us to do by counting to twelve? What will be the result?2–6 M · repeated

Twelve suggests the hours on a clock / months of the year. He wants everyone to stop talking and moving for a moment — no wars, no fishermen harming whales, no salt-gatherers hurting their hands — to introspect. It would bring "an exotic moment", togetherness and understanding.

Q. "Perhaps the Earth can teach us." Explain. Is stillness the same as inactivity?

Personification: the Earth seems dead in winter but is alive under the surface and blooms again. Similarly, silence is not death or total inactivity; it is a pause that renews us. The poet does not want "total inactivity" or wars with "clean clothes" on victory — he wants men to walk with their brothers in peace.

3. A Thing of Beauty — John Keats (from Endymion)

Q. Why is a thing of beauty "a joy for ever"? What are its sources?extract · repeated

Its loveliness increases and never passes into nothingness; it keeps "a bower quiet for us" — a shady, peaceful shelter — giving sweet dreams, health and quiet breathing. Sources: sun, moon, old and young trees, daffodils, clear rills, the musk-rose, grandeur of the dead, lovely tales. They are "an endless fountain of immortal drink" pouring from heaven.

Q. How does beauty help us overcome suffering?

Despite despondence, gloomy days and "unhealthy and o'er-darkened ways", some shape of beauty "moves away the pall" (removes the dark cloth of sadness) from our spirits and gives hope to live.

4. A Roadside Stand — Robert Frost

Q. What do the people at the roadside stand want? How do city people react?2–6 M

The poor farmers want some city money to feel the life of the cities, not charity. Rich travellers in polished cars either pass by without a look, or stop only to complain about the landscape or ask the way. The poet criticises the "greedy good-doers, beneficent beasts of prey" who promise to help but relocate the poor and exploit them. The poet feels the "childish longing" of the stand-owners as an ache he can hardly bear.

5. Aunt Jennifer's Tigers — Adrienne Rich

Q. What do the tigers symbolise? Why are Aunt Jennifer's fingers fluttering?extract · repeated

The tigers — "bright topaz denizens", proud, fearless, "chivalric" (like knights), pacing in "sleek chivalric certainty" — symbolise the freedom and courage Aunt Jennifer lacks. Her fingers flutter and find the needle hard to pull because of "the massive weight of Uncle's wedding band" — the burden and fear of a dominating husband.

Q. What will happen when she dies? What is the poet's tone?

Even in death her "terrified hands" will remain ringed with the ordeals she was mastered by, but the tigers she created will go on "prancing, proud and unafraid" — art outlives the artist and expresses her suppressed desire. Tone: sympathetic towards Aunt Jennifer, critical of patriarchy (not mocking or indifferent).

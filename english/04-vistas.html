Vistas · Supplementary Reader

Six chapters. Q8 extract, Q11 short answers (2 of 3) and Q13 long answer (5 marks). The Enemy and On the Face of It appeared in nearly every paper.

1. The Third Level — Jack Finney

Gist: Charley, 31, claims he found a third level at Grand Central Station with gaslights, brass spittoons, old-style clothes, and an 1894 newspaper — a route to Galesburg, Illinois, a peaceful town of 1894. His psychiatrist friend Sam calls it a "waking-dream wish fulfilment" to escape the insecurity, fear and war of modern life. Sam himself disappears, and Charley finds a first-day cover letter from Sam, now living in Galesburg in 1894.

Q. What does the third level symbolise? What was the psychiatrist's view?2–5 M · repeated

It is an escape from the stress and anxieties of modern life into a simpler, safer past. Sam said it was a wish fulfilment; stamp collecting was also a "temporary refuge from reality". Charley's experience is a mix of fantasy and reality — Sam's letter blurs the line. The old currency he couldn't use stopped him from buying tickets; when he exchanged money, he could never find the third level again.

2. The Tiger King — Kalki

Gist: The Maharaja of Pratibandapuram, told by astrologers he will be killed by the hundredth tiger, sets out to kill a hundred tigers, banning tiger hunting for others. He spends a fortune, nearly loses his throne (gives a British officer's wife expensive diamond rings), marries to get tigers from his in-laws' state. The hundredth tiger, planted by the dewan, survives his shot; later a sliver of a wooden toy tiger pricks his hand → infection → death. Themes: satire on vanity and abuse of power, irony of fate.

Q. How does Kalki use satire and irony? How did the hundredth tiger take its revenge?2–5 M · repeated

Satire on autocratic rulers who misuse power for selfish ends (killing tigers, sacking officials, taxing villagers), and on British officers who are bribed. Irony: the king who killed 99 tigers is killed by a wooden one bought as a birthday gift for his son; the hundredth tiger had only fainted and was killed secretly by the hunters. Fate cannot be defeated.

3. Journey to the End of the Earth — Tishani Doshi

Gist: The author travels with the "Students on Ice" programme to Antarctica aboard the Russian research vessel Akademik Shokalskiy. Antarctica holds 650 million years of history (Gondwana) and is a simple ecosystem with little biodiversity, so small changes have big effects.

Q. Why is Antarctica the ideal place to study Earth's past, present and future? What does the phytoplankton example show?2–5 M · repeated

Its ice cores are a record of the past; it is pristine with no human population; its simple ecosystem shows the effects of global warming quickly. Phytoplankton — single-celled plants — nourish the entire Southern Ocean food chain; any damage to them (e.g. by ozone depletion) would affect all marine life and the global carbon cycle — "take care of the small things and the big things will fall into place". Students go there to understand and act for the future.

4. The Enemy — Pearl S. Buck

Gist: During WWII, Dr Sadao Hoki, a Japanese surgeon trained in America, finds a wounded American POW washed ashore near his house. Despite the risk, he and his wife Hana save him. The servants leave. Sadao informs the ailing General, who promises to send assassins but forgets. Sadao finally helps the American escape in a boat.

Q. How did Sadao resolve the conflict between his duty as a doctor and as a patriot?5 M · every paper

His medical ethics told him to save a life; his patriotism told him to hand over an enemy. He treated the soldier (humanity first), then informed the General (loyalty to country), and when no action came, helped the man escape. He betrayed neither duty. Hana showed compassion by washing the wounded man herself when the servants refused. The General was self-absorbed — he kept Sadao in Japan only because he needed him for his own surgery.

Q. Compare Sadao and Mr Lamb in showing compassion.5 M · comparison

Both rise above prejudice. Sadao saves an enemy despite war hatred; Mr Lamb welcomes the bitter, scarred Derry and helps him see beyond his disability. Both show that humanity transcends barriers of nationality and appearance.

5. On the Face of It — Susan Hill

Gist: Derry, 14, whose face was burnt by acid, hides from people. He enters Mr Lamb's garden; Mr Lamb, who has a tin leg ("Lamey-Lamb"), keeps his gate open and lives positively. Through their talk Derry learns to stop pitying himself; he decides to return despite his mother's objection, but Mr Lamb falls from a ladder and dies.

Q. How does Mr Lamb's garden work as a symbol? How did Mr Lamb help Derry?2–5 M · repeated

The open garden with "weeds" and flowers symbolises an open heart and acceptance — everything has value. Mr Lamb ignores his own disability, talks of bees and crab apples, and teaches Derry that what matters is what's inside, not the face. Children call him Lamey-Lamb but he's not bothered. Derry's transformation: he chooses to see the world and comes back — but finds Mr Lamb dead, a tragic ending.

6. Memories of Childhood — Zitkala-Sa & Bama

The Cutting of My Long Hair (Zitkala-Sa): a Native American girl at a missionary school faces "eating by formula" and forcible shingling of hair (a mark of cowards/mourners in her culture). She hides under a bed but is dragged out; she feels like "one of many little animals driven by a herder".

We Too Are Human Beings (Bama): a Dalit girl in Tamil Nadu sees an elder carrying vadai by a string so as not to "pollute" it. Her brother Annan explains untouchability and advises her to study hard to earn respect.

Q. How did Zitkala-Sa and Bama resist oppression? Compare.5 M · repeated

Zitkala-Sa resisted openly — she hid and fought when her hair was cut, a symbol of losing her cultural identity. Bama's resistance was through education: she studied hard and came first, so that people would come to her on their own. Both stories show discrimination (racial / caste) and how awareness and self-respect help victims fight back.

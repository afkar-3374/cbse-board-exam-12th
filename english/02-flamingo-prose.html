1. The Last Lesson — Alphonse Daudet

Gist: After the Franco-Prussian war (1870–71), Prussia orders that only German be taught in Alsace and Lorraine. M. Hamel gives his last French lesson; Franz, who used to dodge school, suddenly values his language. Themes: linguistic chauvinism, love of mother tongue, procrastination, loss of freedom.

Q. How did Franz's feelings about M. Hamel and school change?2–5 M · very frequent

Franz had found M. Hamel strict and his lessons boring; he even thought of skipping school to watch the Prussian soldiers drill. On learning it was the last French lesson, he felt regret for wasting time and saw his books as old friends. M. Hamel's dignity and sorrow moved him — he forgot the ruler and the scoldings, understood every word of grammar, and realised the worth of his language when it was being taken away.

Q. Why were the village elders sitting in the classroom?2 M

To thank M. Hamel for forty years of faithful service, to show respect to the country that was no longer theirs, and out of regret for not having learnt French properly themselves.

Q. Explain: "When a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison."

Language carries a people's identity and culture. Even under foreign rule, if they preserve their language they keep their unity and the hope of regaining freedom — it is a key to liberation. M. Hamel blames everyone (parents who sent children to work, himself for giving holidays) for "putting off learning till tomorrow".

Linking question: M. Hamel and Gandhi (Indigo) both resist oppression — one through language and identity, the other through non-violent civil disobedience and self-reliance.

2. Lost Spring — Anees Jung

Gist: Two excerpts on child labour. Saheb, a ragpicker from Dhaka living in Seemapuri, Delhi, scrounges garbage barefoot; later works at a tea stall. Mukesh belongs to a bangle-making family in Firozabad but dreams of becoming a motor mechanic. Themes: poverty, exploitation, loss of childhood, the vicious circle of tradition and middlemen.

Q. Why is Saheb's new job described as a loss of freedom?2 M · repeated

At the tea stall he earns ₹800 and meals, but he now carries a steel canister instead of a plastic bag and is no longer his own master. The canister is heavier than the bag; the carefree look of the ragpicker has gone. He has traded freedom for security — the irony of his name, Saheb-e-Alam ("lord of the universe").

Q. How is Mukesh different from the other bangle makers? / How do Saheb and Mukesh respond to poverty?

The bangle makers accept their fate as "God-given lineage" — the vicious circle of sahukars, middlemen, police and politicians crushes initiative. Mukesh dares to dream of driving cars and being a motor mechanic, even planning to walk to the garage. Saheb drifts into dependence; Mukesh shows hope and determination. The author's suggestion of a cooperative is rejected by the young men out of fear of the police and middlemen.

Q. "Garbage to them is gold." Explain.

For the elders, garbage is a means of survival; for the children it is wrapped in wonder — a coin or a rupee found gives hope of finding more.

3. Deep Water — William O. Douglas

Gist: A childhood near-drowning at the Yakima river and later at the YMCA pool (a big boy tossed him into the deep end) left Douglas with a terror of water. He hired an instructor, learnt bit by bit, and finally conquered the fear by swimming alone in lakes. Themes: fear and how to overcome it, will power, "All we have to fear is fear itself" (Roosevelt).

Q. How did Douglas overcome his fear of water?2–5 M · very frequent

He hired an instructor who taught him piece by piece: a rope and pulley around his waist, then breathing (exhale under water, inhale above), then kicking his legs, then putting it all together over seven months. He still felt residual terror, so he tested himself alone at Lake Wentworth, Warm Lake and the Tieton River until the fear left. Overcoming fear was a process of persistence, not one act of courage.

Q. Describe his experience at the YMCA pool. How did it affect him?

A bully threw him into the 9-ft deep end. He went down three times, planned to spring up from the bottom but rose slowly, swallowed water, panicked, and finally fainted — "a blankness". The terror stayed with him for years, ruining canoeing, fishing and swimming. Lesson: fear of death is worse than death; courage lies in facing it.

4. The Rattrap — Selma Lagerlöf

Gist: A poor vagabond peddler believes the world is a rattrap — riches, food and shelter are bait. He robs a kind crofter, gets lost in the forest ("caught in the rattrap himself"), shelters at the Ramsjö ironworks, where the ironmaster mistakes him for an old comrade, Captain von Stahle. Edla, the ironmaster's daughter, treats him with compassion even after the truth is out. Moved, he leaves the money and a rattrap as a Christmas gift. Themes: essential goodness of humans, redemption through love, loneliness.

Q. Why did the peddler sign himself Captain von Stahle?2 M · repeated

Edla treated him like a real captain, so he wanted to be worthy of the title. Signing as Captain von Stahle shows his transformation from a thief into an honest man who has escaped the "rattrap" himself — he says he would have been caught in it had he not been raised to a captain.

Q. Why did the peddler decline the ironmaster's invitation but accept Edla's?

He feared being recognised and arrested (he had the stolen money). Edla's compassionate manner made him feel safe and pitied; she persuaded him with kindness. The ironmaster had invited him under a mistaken identity.

Q. How does the rattrap metaphor work?

Material things are bait that lure people; once tempted they are trapped. The peddler falls into his own metaphor when he steals; Edla's goodness releases him.

5. Indigo — Louis Fischer

Gist: Rajkumar Shukla, a sharecropper from Champaran, persuades Gandhi to come (1917). Under an old contract, peasants had to grow indigo on 15% of land and give it as rent. When synthetic indigo arrived, the British landlords demanded compensation. Gandhi investigates, defies an order to leave, is supported by the crowd, wins an inquiry, and accepts a 25% refund. Themes: civil disobedience, self-reliance, freedom from fear.

Q. Why did Gandhi agree to a 25% refund?2–5 M · asked in many papers

The amount was not important; the principle was. By paying back part of the money, the landlords were forced to surrender part of their prestige and admit they were wrong. It broke the deadlock and showed the peasants they had rights and could stand up to the British. Within a few years the planters left and the land returned to the peasants.

Q. Why is Champaran called the beginning of the freedom struggle? How was self-reliance linked to it?

It was Gandhi's first successful civil disobedience in India; the peasants learnt courage. Gandhi refused C.F. Andrews' help, saying they must rely on themselves (self-reliance). He also worked on cultural and social backwardness — opened schools, improved sanitation and health, with volunteers including Kasturba. "The beginning of the liberation from fear of the British."

6. Poets and Pancakes — Asokamitran

Gist: Humorous memoir of Gemini Studios (Madras, 1940s). "Pancake" was a make-up brand. Kothamangalam Subbu — the boss's No. 2, versatile, inventive, loyal. The office boy who wanted to be a poet/actor. The visit of Stephen Spender, an English poet, whose talk no one understood — "an unexplained mystery". The God That Failed — book of essays by six ex-communists including Spender.

Q. How did Subbu show versatility? Why did others resent him?2–5 M

He gave filmmaking a direction; offered many ideas when the boss was stuck; was a poet, novelist and a fine actor who never coveted the lead role; was charitable and hospitable. His proximity to the boss made others feel he was a sycophant; the office boy blamed him for his own failure.

Q. Why did the make-up room look like a hair-cutting salon? What was the "fiery misery"?

It had incandescent lights and large mirrors on the upper floor. The "fiery misery" was the heat and glare of the lights that made being made up uncomfortable. Make-up men turned any decent-looking person into a "hideously painted monster". The hierarchy among make-up staff also reflected rigid social order.

7. The Interview — Christopher Silvester (Part I) & Umberto Eco (Part II)

Part I: Views on interviews — an art and source of truth vs an invasion (Naipaul felt part of himself lost; Kipling called it immoral; Lewis Carroll had a horror of interviewers). Part II: Mukund Padmanabhan interviews Umberto Eco.

Q. How does Eco find time to do so much? What are "interstices"?2 M · repeated

He uses "empty spaces" (interstices) — the time while waiting for a lift, between tasks — to write. He does not see himself as a novelist but a scholar who writes novels on Sundays; all his work is guided by one philosophical interest.

Q. Why did Eco say he started writing novels by accident? What explains The Name of the Rose's success?

A publisher friend asked non-novelists to write short detective novels; Eco declined, then found he had a story to tell and wrote it for his own satisfaction. Its success is a mystery: probably the medieval setting and detective plot drew readers who wanted difficult reading experiences — he would not have sold so many copies at another time.

8. Going Places — A.R. Barton

Gist: Sophie, a working-class schoolgirl, dreams of owning a boutique, being an actress or a fashion designer, and fantasises about meeting the young footballer Danny Casey. Her friend Jansie is practical; her brother Geoff is quiet; her father is gruff. The meeting with Danny is imaginary; she waits in vain and is left disappointed. Themes: adolescent fantasy vs reality, hero worship, class barriers.

Q. Contrast Sophie and Jansie.2–5 M · repeated

Sophie lives in a world of dreams — boutique, acting, Danny Casey — ignoring that she will work in a biscuit factory. Jansie is realistic, knows they lack money and must take factory jobs, and warns Sophie. Sophie's dreams end in heartbreak because she cannot accept reality; dreaming is fine, but it must be backed by realistic effort.

Q. Why did Sophie want a boutique? Why didn't her father believe her story?

She longed to escape poverty and to have glamour and money. Her father dismissed the story as one of her imaginary tales — her family knew her habit of daydreaming. Geoff's silence and grown-up world made Sophie feel left out.

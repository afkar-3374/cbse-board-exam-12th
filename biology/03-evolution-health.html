1. Origin of life & evidence

Q. Describe Miller's experiment. What did it show?2–3 M

Oparin and Haldane proposed that life arose from non-living organic molecules (chemical evolution). Miller (1953) sparked CH₄, H₂, NH₃ and water vapour at 800 °C in a closed flask and obtained amino acids → showed organic molecules could form abiotically on the early Earth.

2. Darwin, natural selection, mechanisms

Q. Explain Darwin's theory with the finches and with industrial melanism.3 M

  • Organisms overproduce; resources are limited → struggle for existence.
  • Heritable variations exist; individuals with useful variations survive and reproduce more (fitness = reproductive fitness).
  • Over generations, favourable traits accumulate → new species.

Darwin's finches (Galapagos Islands): from one seed-eating ancestor, many beak types evolved for different foods → adaptive radiation. (Australian marsupials are another example; placental and marsupial mammals show convergent evolution.)

Industrial melanism (England): before industrialisation, white moths dominated on lichen-covered trees; after soot darkened the bark, dark moths were less visible to birds and increased. Natural selection acts on existing variation — individuals do not change colour during their lifetime.

Antibiotic/insecticide resistance: resistant variants already present are favoured and increase in frequency (not directed mutation).

Q. Types of natural selection; genetic drift and founder effect.1–2 M

Finches with large and small beaks favoured, medium ones struggle → disruptive selection.

Genetic drift: random change in allele frequency, strong in small populations. Founder effect: a few individuals found a new population with different allele frequencies. Bottleneck: a disaster randomly kills most individuals.

Factors affecting HW equilibrium: gene migration (gene flow), genetic drift, mutation, recombination, natural selection.

3. Hardy–Weinberg principle

Q. State the Hardy–Weinberg principle. A recessive disorder affects 4% of a population of 20,00,000. How many are carriers?2 M · 12 papers

In a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration, drift or selection, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation.

p + q = 1,   p² + 2pq + q² = 1
  • q² = 0.04 ⇒ q = 0.2, p = 0.8 ⇒ 2pq = 0.32 ⇒ carriers = 0.32 × 20,00,000 = 6,40,000.
  • q² = 0.09 ⇒ q = 0.3, p = 0.7 ⇒ carriers 2pq = 0.42 = 42%.
  • q² = 0.16 ⇒ q = 0.4 ⇒ 2pq = 0.48.
  • p = 0.7, q = 0.3 ⇒ heterozygotes 0.42.

If frequencies change → evolution is happening. The deviation measures the extent of evolutionary change.

Q. Describe the life cycle of Plasmodium. Why do malarial symptoms recur?3–5 M · 8 papers

  • Enters human as sporozoites (infectious form) by the bite of an infected female Anopheles.
  • Multiplies asexually in liver cells, then in RBCs.
  • Bursting RBCs release haemozoin — toxin causing chill and high fever recurring every 3–4 days (each cycle of RBC rupture).
  • Some parasites become gametocytes; a mosquito sucking blood takes them up. Fertilisation (sexual reproduction) occurs in the mosquito's gut; sporozoites move to its salivary glands.

Hosts: human = intermediate (asexual); mosquito = definitive (sexual).

5. Immunity

Q. Innate immunity: name the four barriers with examples.2 M

  1. Physical: skin, mucus coating of respiratory/gut/urogenital tracts.
  2. Physiological: stomach acid, saliva, tears.
  3. Cellular: neutrophils (PMNL), monocytes, NK cells, macrophages.
  4. Cytokine: interferons from virus-infected cells protect nearby cells.

Q. Explain acquired immunity — primary/secondary response, humoral vs cell-mediated, active vs passive.3 M

  • Pathogen-specific and has memory. First encounter → primary response (low intensity); second encounter → highly intensified secondary (anamnestic) response.
  • B-lymphocytes → antibodies in blood (humoral immunity). T-lymphocytes → cell-mediated immunity, responsible for graft/organ rejection.
  • Antibody = H₂L₂ (two heavy, two light chains). Types IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG.
  • Active: body makes its own antibodies (infection, vaccination). Passive: ready-made antibodies given (colostrum IgA, foetus via placenta IgG, anti-tetanus serum, snake antivenom).
  • A person recovered from measles → active natural immunity; anti-tetanus serum → passive.

Vaccination: inactivated/weakened pathogen or antigenic protein → primary response and memory B and T cells → quick secondary response on real infection. Recombinant (2nd-generation) vaccines, e.g. hepatitis B from yeast — safer because they contain only antigen. An antibody against one pathogen does not protect against an unrelated one because antigen–antibody binding is specific.

Allergy: exaggerated response to allergens (dust, pollen) via IgE; mast cells release histamine and serotonin → sneezing, watery eyes. Treated with antihistamines, adrenaline, steroids. Autoimmunity: body attacks self cells (rheumatoid arthritis).

Lymphoid organs: primary — bone marrow, thymus; secondary — spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches, MALT (about 50% of lymphoid tissue, lining major tracts).

6. AIDS & cancer

Q. Describe how HIV multiplies in the body. How is it diagnosed and prevented?3–4 M case study

  1. HIV (a retrovirus with RNA genome) enters macrophages.
  2. Reverse transcriptase makes viral DNA from RNA; it integrates into host DNA.
  3. Macrophages keep producing virus (act as HIV factory).
  4. HIV enters helper T-lymphocytes (TH), replicates and kills them → TH count falls → immunodeficiency → opportunistic infections (Mycobacterium, Toxoplasma, fungi).

Diagnosis: ELISA (Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay). Treatment: anti-retroviral drugs (prolong life, not cure).

Transmission: sexual contact with an infected person, contaminated blood transfusion, shared needles, infected mother to child through placenta. Not by touch, sharing food, handshakes or classrooms — so avoiding an HIV-positive classmate is unjustified; treat them with dignity and empathy.

Prevention (WHO/NACO): safe blood, disposable needles, free condoms, controlling drug abuse, awareness, regular check-ups.

Q. How do cancer cells differ from normal cells? Distinguish benign and malignant tumours. How is cancer detected and treated?3–5 M

  • Normal cells show contact inhibition; cancer cells lose it and divide uncontrollably.
  • Benign: stays confined, little damage. Malignant: proliferates, invades and spreads via blood to distant sites — metastasis (the most feared property).
  • Causes (carcinogens): ionising radiation (X-rays, γ), non-ionising (UV), chemicals (tobacco smoke), oncogenic viruses (with viral oncogenes); cellular oncogenes (proto-oncogenes) when activated.
  • Detection: biopsy and histopathology, blood/bone-marrow tests (leukaemia), radiography, CT, MRI, antibodies against cancer antigens, molecular biology to detect genes.
  • Treatment: surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy (biological response modifiers like α-interferon).

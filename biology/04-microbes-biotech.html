2. Sewage treatment & biogas

Q. Explain primary and secondary treatment of sewage. What are flocs? Why is BOD measured?3 M

  1. Primary (physical): sequential filtration and sedimentation remove floating debris, grit; solids settle as primary sludge.
  2. Secondary (biological): primary effluent goes to large aeration tanks, agitated with air → aerobic microbes form flocs (bacteria + fungal filaments, mesh-like). They consume organic matter, lowering BOD.
  3. Effluent goes to a settling tank; the sediment is activated sludge. A part is returned as inoculum; the rest goes to anaerobic sludge digesters, where bacteria produce biogas (CH₄, H₂S, CO₂).

BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) = amount of O₂ consumed if all organic matter in one litre of water were oxidised by bacteria. Higher BOD = more polluted. It is monitored to judge how well the water has been treated. Ganga and Yamuna Action Plans (Ministry of Environment and Forests).

Q. How is biogas produced? Why is cattle dung slurry added?2–3 M

Biogas (mainly methane) is produced anaerobically by methanogens (e.g. Methanobacterium) found in cattle rumen and dung. Dung slurry is added as it is rich in methanogens. Plant: concrete tank (10–15 ft deep) with a floating cover that rises as gas collects; outlet for spent slurry (used as fertiliser). Technology developed by IARI and KVIC.

3. Biocontrol & biofertilisers

Q. Why do organic farmers prefer biocontrol? Give examples.3 M

  • Chemical pesticides are toxic to non-target organisms and pollute; biocontrol is species-specific and keeps ecological balance. Organic farmers aim to keep pests at manageable levels, not eradicate them (eradication would also remove beneficial insects that depend on them).
  • Ladybird beetles → aphids; dragonflies → mosquitoes.
  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) → butterfly caterpillars.
  • Trichoderma (fungus) → plant pathogens in the root zone.
  • Baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus, NPV) → insects; narrow spectrum, no effect on non-target organisms — ideal for IPM.

In the case data (organic plot: 42 pests, 24 beneficial insects, 7% damage, 8 plant species vs chemical plot: 15 pests, 6 beneficial, 5% damage, 3 species), the organic plot keeps damage low while conserving biodiversity — a zero pest count is not the goal.

Q. What are biofertilisers? Give examples.2 M

  • Rhizobium — symbiotic N₂-fixation in root nodules of legumes (reduces need for nitrogen fertilisers).
  • Azotobacter, Azospirillum — free-living N₂-fixers.
  • Mycorrhiza (e.g. Glomus) — fungus with roots: absorbs phosphorus, resistance to root pathogens, drought and salinity tolerance.
  • Cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) — N₂-fixation in paddy fields; add organic matter.

4. Tools of rDNA technology

Q. What are restriction endonucleases? Explain their naming, palindromes and sticky ends.2–3 M · 15 papers

Enzymes that cut DNA at specific recognition sequences ("molecular scissors").

Naming — EcoRI: E = genus Escherichia, co = species coli, R = strain RY13, I = first enzyme isolated from that strain.

Palindrome: sequence that reads the same on both strands in the 5′→3′ direction:

5′-G↓AATTC-3′
3′-CTTAA↑G-5′

EcoRI cuts between G and A on both strands, leaving single-stranded overhangs — sticky ends — which form H-bonds with complementary ends cut by the same enzyme. DNA ligase then joins them.

Both vector and foreign DNA must be cut with the same enzyme so their sticky ends are complementary. A vector should have a single recognition site for the enzyme used — more sites would cut the vector into several pieces.

Q. How are host cells made competent to take up DNA?2–3 M

  • Bacteria: treat with divalent cations (Ca²⁺) to increase membrane permeability → incubate on ice → heat shock (42 °C) → back on ice. DNA (hydrophilic) can then enter.
  • Animal cells: microinjection into the nucleus.
  • Plant cells: biolistics/gene gun — gold or tungsten particles coated with DNA.
  • Disarmed pathogen vectors: Agrobacterium (Ti plasmid modified so it is no longer pathogenic but still transfers T-DNA), retroviruses.

5. Cloning vectors: pBR322

Q. Draw pBR322. State the features of a good cloning vector. How are recombinants selected?2–5 M · 8 papers

  • ori: origin of replication — needed for replication and controls copy number.
  • Selectable markers: ampR, tetR (antibiotic resistance genes) — identify transformants.
  • Cloning sites: single recognition sites for common enzymes.
  • rop: codes for proteins involved in plasmid replication (controls copy number).
  • Small size; easy to isolate.

Insertional inactivation: insert foreign DNA into BamHI (inside tetR) → recombinants lose tetracycline resistance but keep ampicillin resistance. Plate on ampicillin, then replica-plate on tetracycline: recombinants grow on amp but not on tet.

Insert at PstI (inside ampR): non-transformants grow on neither; non-recombinant transformants grow on both; recombinants grow only on tetracycline.

Blue–white selection: insert into the β-galactosidase gene → recombinant colonies are white (no colour with chromogenic substrate); non-recombinants are blue. Easier than replica plating.

6. Processes of rDNA technology

Q. How is DNA isolated and precipitated?2 M

Break cells with enzymes: lysozyme (bacteria), cellulase (plant), chitinase (fungi). Remove RNA with ribonuclease and proteins with protease. Add chilled ethanol → purified DNA precipitates as fine threads, spooled out.

Q. Explain gel electrophoresis.2–5 M

  • DNA is negatively charged (phosphate groups) → moves to the anode.
  • Matrix: agarose (from sea weed) — sieving effect: smaller fragments move farther.
  • Visualised by staining with ethidium bromide + UV light (orange bands).
  • Bands are cut out and DNA extracted — elution.

Q. Explain the steps of PCR. Why does amplification become exponential?3–4 M · 10 papers

PCR (Kary Mullis) amplifies a specific DNA segment in vitro. Needs template DNA, two oligonucleotide primers (one set per cycle), dNTPs and a thermostable DNA polymerase — Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus, which survives denaturation temperatures.

Each new copy becomes a template in the next cycle, so the number doubles every cycle (2ⁿ) → exponential. Applications: diagnosing infections (HIV), detecting mutations, DNA fingerprinting, cloning.

Q. Why are stirred-tank bioreactors preferred? Why aseptic conditions and control of pH, temperature, O₂?2–4 M

Bioreactors (100–1000 L) grow large volumes of recombinant cells. The stirred-tank type has a curved base and a stirrer for mixing and oxygen distribution; a sparger bubbles sterile air. It also has foam control, temperature and pH control, and sampling ports. Contamination competes with the useful microbe and lowers yield, so aseptic conditions are essential. Optimal pH, temperature and O₂ keep enzymes active and growth maximal (product mostly formed in the log phase). Downstream processing: separation, purification, formulation with preservatives, clinical trials and quality control.

7. Applications

Q. How is Bt cotton resistant to bollworms? Why doesn't the toxin kill the bacterium?3 M · very frequent

Bacillus thuringiensis produces Cry proteins as an inactive protoxin (crystals). In the alkaline gut of the insect larva, the protoxin is solubilised and activated; it binds to midgut epithelial cells and creates pores → cells swell and lyse → insect dies.

Genes: cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms; cryIAb controls corn borer. The bacterium is not harmed because the toxin is inactive inside it (protoxin form) and needs alkaline pH to activate.

Q. Explain RNA interference (RNAi) for nematode resistance in tobacco.3 M

Nematode Meloidogyne incognita infects tobacco roots. Nematode-specific genes were introduced via Agrobacterium vectors so that the plant makes both sense and antisense RNA. These form dsRNA, which initiates RNAi and silences the specific mRNA of the nematode → the parasite cannot survive in the transgenic host.

It is "silencing" (not removal) because the gene stays in the DNA; only its mRNA is degraded/blocked, so translation stops.

For a single strand 5′-ATGGGGCTC-3′: sense RNA 5′-AUGGGGCUC-3′, antisense RNA 3′-UACCCCGAG-5′.

Q. How was human insulin produced by rDNA technology (Humulin)? Why did it take long?2–3 M

Insulin is made as pro-insulin with an extra C peptide, which is removed in mature insulin (A and B chains linked by disulphide bridges). Getting E. coli to make and process pro-insulin was difficult. Eli Lilly (1983) prepared two DNA sequences for chains A and B, inserted them into plasmids of E. coli separately, produced the chains separately, then combined them by disulphide bonds. GM insulin is structurally identical to human insulin (it has no C chain) and does not cause allergy like animal insulin.

Q. What is gene therapy? Explain with ADA deficiency.2–5 M · 13 papers

Gene therapy corrects a gene defect diagnosed in a person by introducing a functional gene into cells.

First used (1990) on a 4-year-old girl with adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency — the ADA gene deletion causes immunodeficiency.

  1. Lymphocytes taken from the patient's blood and grown in culture.
  2. Functional ADA cDNA introduced into them using a retroviral vector.
  3. Cells returned to the patient. Periodic infusion needed as lymphocytes are not immortal.

Permanent cure: introduce the gene into cells at early embryonic stages / bone marrow stem cells. Drugs only manage symptoms, while gene therapy targets the genetic cause.

Q. Other applications and issues.1–3 M

  • Transgenic animals: study disease, biological products (Rosie the cow, α-lactalbumin-enriched milk), vaccine and chemical safety testing.
  • Molecular diagnosis: PCR, ELISA (antigen–antibody), recombinant DNA probes.
  • GEAC = Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee — approves GM research and release (e.g. GM brinjal) for safety.
  • Biopiracy: use of bio-resources/traditional knowledge without authorisation or compensation (e.g. basmati patent, turmeric, neem). Indian Patents Bill amended.
  • Somatic hybridisation: fuse protoplasts of incompatible species, e.g. pomato.

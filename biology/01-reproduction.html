1. Anther, microsporogenesis, pollen

Q. Draw a TS of a mature anther. Label tapetum and endothecium and give their functions.3 M

  • Endothecium: protection and helps in dehiscence of the anther to release pollen.
  • Tapetum: innermost layer, nourishes developing pollen grains; cells have dense cytoplasm and more than one nucleus.

Microsporogenesis: each microspore mother cell (2n) divides by meiosis → tetrad of 4 microspores (n) → each becomes a pollen grain. 250 MMCs → 1000 microspores → 1000 pollen grains.

Q. Draw a labelled diagram of a mature pollen grain. Why is pollen well preserved as fossils?2 M

Exine is made of sporopollenin, the most resistant organic material known — withstands high temperatures, strong acids and alkalis, and no enzyme degrades it. Germ pores lack sporopollenin. Intine is made of cellulose and pectin. At shedding, pollen is 2-celled (vegetative + generative) in most angiosperms (60%) or 3-celled. Pollen can be stored for years in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) in pollen banks for crop breeding.

3. Pollination, outbreeding, hybridisation

Q. Distinguish chasmogamous and cleistogamous flowers. Which is advantageous to the species?3–5 M

Chasmogamous: open flowers with exposed anthers and stigma — cross or self pollination. Cleistogamous: never open; anthers and stigma lie close → autogamy only; assured seed set even without pollinators (Viola, Oxalis, Commelina bear both).

Chasmogamy is more beneficial to the species because cross-pollination brings genetic variation; cleistogamy ensures seeds but reduces variation.

Types: autogamy (same flower), geitonogamy (another flower of the same plant — genetically like autogamy), xenogamy (different plant — true cross).

Q. List the outbreeding devices in flowering plants.3 M

  1. Dichogamy: pollen release and stigma receptivity not synchronised (protandry / protogyny).
  2. Herkogamy: anther and stigma at different positions.
  3. Self-incompatibility: genetic mechanism preventing pollen from germinating on the same plant's stigma.
  4. Unisexual flowers: monoecious (castor, maize) prevents autogamy; dioecious (papaya) prevents both autogamy and geitonogamy.

Q. Steps of artificial hybridisation (e.g. in tomato).3 M

  1. Select parents with desired traits.
  2. Emasculation: remove anthers from the bisexual flower bud before they dehisce.
  3. Bagging: cover with butter paper bag to prevent contamination by unwanted pollen.
  4. When the stigma is receptive, dust pollen from the chosen male parent.
  5. Rebag and allow fruit to develop.

Unisexual female flowers need no emasculation — only bagging.

Q. Explain sexual deceit/pseudocopulation in Ophrys. What type of evolution is this?3–5 M

One petal of the Ophrys flower resembles the female bee in size, colour and markings. The male bee "pseudocopulates" with it and gets dusted with pollen, which it transfers to another flower. This is mutualism and co-evolution — if the female bee's colour pattern changes even slightly, pollination success falls unless the flower evolves too.

4. Double fertilisation, seed, apomixis

Q. What is double fertilisation? Name the products.2–3 M

Syngamy: male gamete + egg → zygote (2n)  |  Triple fusion: male gamete + 2 polar nuclei → PEN (3n)

Both occur in the embryo sac → unique to angiosperms. The primary endosperm nucleus develops into endosperm (nourishes the embryo) before the zygote divides. If one male gamete fails to fuse with the egg but triple fusion occurs, endosperm may form but no zygote.

Terms: perisperm = persistent nucellus (black pepper, beet); endosperm used up → non-albuminous seeds (pea, groundnut); persistent → albuminous (wheat, castor). Coconut water = free-nuclear endosperm.

Q. Define apomixis, parthenocarpy and polyembryony. How is apomixis useful to farmers?2–3 M

  • Apomixis: seed formation without fertilisation (Asteraceae, grasses).
  • Parthenocarpy: fruit formation without fertilisation, usually seedless (banana) — different from apomixis.
  • Polyembryony: more than one embryo in a seed (citrus, mango) — from nucellar cells.

Hybrid seeds must be bought every year because hybrid characters segregate in the next generation. Apomictic hybrids would keep hybrid traits for many years — cheaper for farmers.

5. Male reproductive system & spermatogenesis

Q. Explain spermatogenesis. State the roles of Sertoli and Leydig cells. Distinguish spermiogenesis and spermiation.3–5 M

  • Sertoli cells: in seminiferous tubules — nourish germ cells (they do not secrete testosterone).
  • Leydig (interstitial) cells: between tubules — secrete androgens (testosterone).
  • Spermiogenesis: transformation of spermatids into spermatozoa. Spermiation: release of sperms from the seminiferous tubules.
  • Control: GnRH → FSH (acts on Sertoli cells → spermiogenesis) and LH (acts on Leydig cells → androgens).
  • Testes are in the scrotum to keep them 2–2.5 °C below body temperature, needed for spermatogenesis.

Sperm parts: head (acrosome with enzymes to penetrate the ovum, nucleus), middle piece (mitochondria for energy), tail.

6. Oogenesis

Q. Describe oogenesis. How many polar bodies and ova form from 200 primary oocytes?3 M

Oogonia form in the foetal ovary → primary oocytes (2n) arrested in prophase I → at puberty, in tertiary follicles, meiosis I is completed: unequal division → large secondary oocyte + first polar body. Meiosis II is completed only after sperm entry → ovum + second polar body.

The unequal division keeps most of the cytoplasm in one cell (the ovum needs food for the embryo). 200 primary oocytes → 200 ova and 600 polar bodies (if the first polar body divides) = 800 meiotic products.

Follicle sequence: primary → secondary → tertiary (antrum) → Graafian follicle → ovulation → corpus luteum.

7. Menstrual cycle

Q. Describe the phases of the menstrual cycle with the hormone graph. What is the LH surge?3–5 M · 15 papers

  1. Menstrual phase (days 1–5): endometrium breaks down due to fall in progesterone when the egg is not fertilised.
  2. Follicular phase: FSH and LH rise → follicles develop and secrete estrogen → endometrium regenerates.
  3. Ovulation (~day 14): LH surge (and FSH peak) causes rupture of the Graafian follicle and release of the secondary oocyte.
  4. Luteal phase: ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes large amounts of progesterone to maintain the endometrium for implantation.

No fertilisation → corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → menstruation. Menstruation does not occur right after ovulation because the corpus luteum keeps progesterone high for about two weeks. During pregnancy, hCG maintains the corpus luteum, so cycles stop. Menarche = first menstruation; menopause ≈ 50 years.

8. Fertilisation, implantation, parturition

Q. Trace the events from fertilisation to implantation. Draw a blastocyst.3–5 M

Fertilisation occurs in the ampullary region of the fallopian tube; the sperm contacts the zona pellucida, acrosome enzymes help entry, and blocks prevent polyspermy. Zygote divides (cleavage) → 2, 4, 8, 16 cells (morula) → blastocyst, which implants in the endometrium (implantation).

Trophoblast attaches to the endometrium and forms the placenta (with the uterine tissue); inner cell mass differentiates into the embryo (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) — these are stem cells.

Placenta: exchange of O₂, nutrients, removal of CO₂ and wastes; endocrine — hCG, hPL, estrogens, progestogens. Relaxin from the ovary in late pregnancy.

Parturition: signals from the fully developed foetus and placenta → mild uterine contractions (foetal ejection reflex) → oxytocin release from the maternal pituitary → stronger contractions → more oxytocin (positive feedback) → birth. Doctors give oxytocin to induce labour.

If the embryo fails to attach, implantation has failed.

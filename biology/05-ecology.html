1. Population attributes & age pyramids

Q. Draw the three types of age pyramids. What do they indicate?2 M

Population density measured by counts, per cent cover, biomass, or indirectly — tiger census uses pug marks and faecal pellets. Other attributes: birth rate (natality), death rate (mortality), sex ratio.

Nt+1 = Nt + [(B + I) − (D + E)].

2. Population growth

Q. Compare exponential and logistic growth with equations and curves. Which is more realistic?2–5 M · 11 papers

Exponential: dN/dt = rN,   Nt = N₀e^{rt}
Logistic (Verhulst–Pearl): dN/dt = rN (K − N)/K

Exponential growth occurs when resources are unlimited (a population with abundant resources; Darwin's elephant calculation). It is unrealistic because resources are always finite. In nature, growth slows as N approaches the carrying capacity K (lag, log, deceleration, asymptote) — logistic is more realistic. A population that switches from J to S has met resource limitation / competition.

r = intrinsic rate of natural increase (Norway rat 0.015, flour beetle 0.12; India 1981 0.0205).

  • Gause's competitive exclusion principle: two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot coexist; the inferior one is eliminated. After removing the superior competitor, the other expands its range — competitive release.
  • Resource partitioning (MacArthur's warblers) reduces competition.
  • Brood parasitism: koel lays eggs in a crow's nest; eggs mimic the host's eggs.
  • Plant defences against herbivores: thorns (cactus, acacia), chemicals — Calotropis produces cardiac glycosides; nicotine, caffeine, quinine.
  • Parasite adaptations: loss of unnecessary organs, hooks and suckers, loss of digestive system, high reproductive capacity, intermediate hosts.
  • Prey defences: camouflage, monarch butterfly distasteful (eats a poisonous weed as a caterpillar).

4. Ecosystem: productivity, decomposition, energy flow

Not in Pre-Board 1 — board-pattern questions.

Q. Define GPP, NPP and secondary productivity.2 M

NPP = GPP − R (respiration losses)

Primary productivity is the rate of biomass production by plants (g m⁻² yr⁻¹). NPP is available to consumers. Secondary productivity = rate of new organic matter formation by consumers. Annual NPP of the biosphere ≈ 170 billion tons (dry weight); oceans only 55 despite covering 70% of the surface.

Q. Describe the steps of decomposition and the factors affecting it.3 M

  1. Fragmentation by detritivores (earthworm).
  2. Leaching of water-soluble nutrients into soil.
  3. Catabolism by bacterial and fungal enzymes.
  4. Humification → humus (dark, resistant, slow to decompose, reservoir of nutrients).
  5. Mineralisation → inorganic nutrients released.

Fast when detritus is rich in nitrogen and sugars, and in warm, moist, aerobic conditions; slow with lignin/chitin, low temperature, anaerobic conditions.

Q. Energy flow and the 10% law.2 M

Plants capture only 2–10% of PAR (photosynthetically active radiation = ~50% of incident solar radiation). Energy flow is unidirectional (sun → producers → consumers). Only about 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level (Lindeman); the rest is lost as heat. Hence food chains are short (3–4 levels). Grazing food chain starts with producers; detritus food chain starts with dead organic matter (major conduit in terrestrial ecosystems).

5. Ecological pyramids

Q. Draw an inverted pyramid of biomass. Why is the pyramid of energy always upright?2–3 M

Energy is lost at each level (only ~10% transferred), so the energy at a higher level can never exceed that below. Biomass can be inverted in the sea because phytoplankton have small standing crop but reproduce very fast. Number pyramid can be inverted for a tree supporting many insects. Limitations: a species may occupy several trophic levels; saprophytes are not included.

6. Biodiversity & conservation

Not in Pre-Board 1 — board-pattern questions.

Q. Explain the species–area relationship.2–3 M

log S = log C + Z log A

Alexander von Humboldt: species richness increases with area up to a limit. Z (slope) is 0.1–0.2 for most areas, but 0.6–1.2 for very large areas like whole continents.

Q. What are the causes of biodiversity loss ("evil quartet")? Explain the rivet popper hypothesis.3 M

  1. Habitat loss and fragmentation — most important (tropical rainforests reduced from 14% to 6% of land).
  2. Over-exploitation — Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon.
  3. Alien species invasions — Nile perch in Lake Victoria; Parthenium, Lantana, water hyacinth; African catfish.
  4. Co-extinctions — a host and its obligate parasite; plant and pollinator.

Rivet popper hypothesis (Paul Ehrlich): an ecosystem is like an aeroplane held by rivets (species). Losing a few rivets may not matter, but losing more — especially key rivets on the wings (keystone species) — threatens the whole system.

Why conserve: narrowly utilitarian (food, medicines, bioprospecting), broadly utilitarian (O₂ — Amazon 20%, pollination, aesthetic), ethical.

Q. Distinguish in situ and ex situ conservation. What are biodiversity hotspots?2–3 M

  • In situ (in natural habitat): biosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, sacred groves (Khasi and Jaintia hills, Aravalli, Western Ghats, Bastar).
  • Hotspots: regions with high species richness and high endemism, under threat. 34 worldwide; 3 cover India: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Himalaya. They cover <2% of land but could reduce extinctions by ~30%.
  • Ex situ (outside habitat): zoos, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks, seed banks, cryopreservation of gametes, tissue culture.
  • Earth Summit Rio (1992), World Summit Johannesburg (2002).

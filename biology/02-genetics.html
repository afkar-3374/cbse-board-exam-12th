1. Mendel's laws & crosses

Q. A tall, round (TTRR) pea is crossed with dwarf, wrinkled (ttrr). Give F1 and F2 and state the laws proved.5 M

F1: all TtRr — tall, round. F1 gametes: TR, Tr, tR, tr (each ¼) → proves law of segregation (alleles separate) and law of independent assortment (pairs assort independently).

2. Incomplete dominance, codominance, ABO

Q. Explain incomplete dominance with snapdragon, and codominance with ABO blood groups.3 M

Incomplete dominance: RR (red) × rr (white) → Rr pink. F2: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white (phenotype = genotype ratio). Snapdragon (Antirrhinum) — not codominance.

Codominance: both alleles express fully in the heterozygote: IAIB → AB blood group. Gene I has 3 alleles (multiple allelism): IA, IB (codominant), i (recessive).

A × B parents with an O child (asked in 7 papers): parents must be IAi and IBi → children: IAIB (AB), IAi (A), IBi (B), ii (O), each ¼. So an O child is perfectly possible.

Pleiotropy: one gene, many effects (phenylketonuria). Polygenic inheritance: many genes, one trait (human skin colour, height).

3. Chromosomal theory, linkage, mapping

Q. Who proposed the chromosomal theory of inheritance? Explain Morgan's linkage experiments.3–5 M

Sutton and Boveri: chromosomes, like genes, occur in pairs, segregate at gamete formation and assort independently — so genes are on chromosomes. Morgan verified it experimentally with Drosophila.

Morgan: dihybrid crosses with X-linked genes gave far more parental types than expected.

  • Yellow body × white eye: 98.7% parental, 1.3% recombinant (tightly linked).
  • White eye × miniature wing: 62.8% parental, 37.2% recombinant (loosely linked).

Linkage = physical association of genes on a chromosome. Recombination frequency ∝ distance between genes. Alfred Sturtevant used it to map genes.

Mapping: P–Q = 20, Q–R = 12, P–R = 8 → P–R + R–Q = 8 + 12 = 20 = P–Q ⇒ order P — R — Q. If a crossover occurs between P and Q, the pair P–R is more likely to stay linked, because P and R are closer (8 map units) than Q and R (12), so a crossover is less likely to fall between them.

5. Genetic disorders

Q. Sex-linked recessive inheritance: haemophilia and colour blindness. Why are males affected more?2–3 M

Genes are on the X chromosome. Males (XY) have only one X → one recessive allele is enough. Females need two copies, so they are usually carriers.

Carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) × normal father (XᴴY): sons 50% affected, daughters 50% carriers (none affected). Probability a son is affected = 50%.

Normal parents with an affected child (autosomal recessive): both parents are carriers (Aa × Aa) → next child affected with probability ¼.

Autosomal dominant disorder: appears in every generation; an affected heterozygous parent passes it on with 50% chance (myotonic dystrophy).

6. DNA structure & the search for genetic material

Q. Describe the double-helix model. If A = 30%, find C.2–3 M

  • Two antiparallel polynucleotide strands (5′→3′ and 3′→5′), sugar-phosphate backbone outside, bases inside.
  • A pairs with T by 2 H-bonds; G with C by 3 H-bonds. A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine → uniform width (20 Å).
  • Pitch 3.4 nm, 10 bp per turn, 0.34 nm between base pairs. Right-handed.
  • Chargaff: A = T, G = C. A = 30% ⇒ T = 30%, G = C = 20%.
  • Nucleotide = base + sugar (N-glycosidic bond) + phosphate (phosphoester); nucleotides joined by 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds. The free 3′-OH end is the 3′ end.

Q. Describe the Hershey–Chase experiment. What did it prove?3 M

  1. Phages grown with ³²P (labels DNA) and others with ³⁵S (labels protein).
  2. Each set infected E. coli; blending removed phage coats; centrifugation separated bacteria (pellet) from viral coats (supernatant).
  3. ³²P was found inside the bacteria; ³⁵S stayed in the supernatant (viral coats).

Conclusion: DNA, not protein, is the genetic material.

Griffith's transformation (S and R Streptococcus) showed a "transforming principle"; Avery, MacLeod and McCarty identified it as DNA.

After generation 1, every molecule has one old (¹⁵N) and one new (¹⁴N) strand → no fully heavy DNA. In generation 2 the hybrid molecules give one hybrid and one light each. This proves semi-conservative replication.

Q. Draw a replication fork. Why is replication continuous on one strand and discontinuous on the other?5 M

DNA polymerase can add nucleotides only in the 5′→3′ direction. On the template with 3′→5′ polarity, synthesis is continuous (leading strand). On the other template, it is made in short Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase (lagging strand).

Replication happens in small forks (not all at once) because separating the whole double helix needs too much energy. Starts at the origin of replication (ori); energy from deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs), which act as both substrate and energy source.

8. Transcription & processing

Q. Draw a transcription unit. Given a coding strand, write the mRNA. Why is only one strand transcribed?2–5 M

Promoter is towards the 5′ end (upstream) of the coding strand; terminator at the 3′ end. RNA polymerase uses the 3′→5′ strand as template.

mRNA = coding strand with U for T, same polarity. Coding 5′-ATGCACGA-3′ → mRNA 5′-AUGCACGA-3′. mRNA 5′-AUGGCGGAGUGGCGA-3′ → template 3′-TACCGCCTCACCGCT-5′.

Only one strand is transcribed because two complementary RNAs would pair to form dsRNA and no protein would be made; also both strands would code for different proteins, complicating the machinery.

Processing in eukaryotes (hnRNA → mRNA): splicing — introns removed and exons joined; capping — methyl guanosine triphosphate added at 5′ end; tailing — poly-A tail (200–300 A) at 3′ end. In bacteria, RNA polymerase uses σ factor for initiation and ρ factor for termination; in eukaryotes RNA pol I (rRNA), II (hnRNA), III (tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNA).

9. Genetic code & translation

Q. State the salient features of the genetic code.2–3 M

  • Triplet: 64 codons; 61 code for amino acids, 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • Unambiguous and specific: one codon → one amino acid.
  • Degenerate: some amino acids have more than one codon.
  • Read 5′→3′ on mRNA, contiguously (no punctuation).
  • Nearly universal. AUG = start codon and methionine.

Mutations: substitution may be silent, missense (sickle-cell) or nonsense (creates a stop). Insertion/deletion of one base causes a frameshift — changes every codon after it, so it is usually more harmful.

tRNA = adapter molecule: anticodon loop reads the codon, amino-acid acceptor end carries the amino acid. Translation: charging (aminoacylation) → initiation at AUG → elongation (peptide bond by ribozyme in the 23S rRNA) → termination at the stop codon. UTRs on both sides of mRNA help efficient translation.

10. Lac operon

Q. Draw and explain the lac operon in the presence and absence of lactose. Why is it negatively regulated?3–5 M · 9 papers

Components: regulator gene i (codes repressor), promoter p, operator o, structural genes z (β-galactosidase — splits lactose), y (permease — lactose entry), a (transacetylase).

No lactose: repressor binds the operator and prevents RNA polymerase from transcribing → operon OFF.

Lactose present: lactose (inducer) binds the repressor, changing its shape so it cannot bind the operator → RNA polymerase transcribes z, y, a → enzymes made → operon ON.

It is negative regulation because the repressor switches it off. If the i gene is removed/inactivated → no repressor → enzymes made continuously (constitutive). If the promoter is mutated → no transcription even with lactose.

11. HGP & DNA fingerprinting

Q. State the goals and salient features of the Human Genome Project. What are ESTs?2–3 M

  • Human genome ≈ 3164.7 million bp; ~20,000–25,000 genes; less than 2% codes for proteins; large repetitive sequences; chromosome 1 has the most genes (2968), Y the fewest (231); ~1.4 million SNPs.
  • Approaches: Expressed Sequence Tags — identifying all genes expressed as RNA; and sequence annotation — sequencing the whole genome and assigning functions.
  • Vectors used: BAC and YAC. Automated sequencers using Sanger's method.

Q. Explain DNA fingerprinting and why VNTRs are used.3–5 M

VNTRs (variable number tandem repeats, satellite DNA) differ in copy number from person to person but are inherited → unique pattern; they are non-coding, so variation is high (polymorphism).

  1. Isolate DNA.
  2. Digest with restriction endonucleases.
  3. Separate fragments by gel electrophoresis.
  4. Transfer (blot) to a nylon/nitrocellulose membrane (Southern blotting).
  5. Hybridise with labelled VNTR probe.
  6. Detect by autoradiography.

Uses: forensic identification, paternity disputes, population and evolutionary studies. Developed by Alec Jeffreys.
